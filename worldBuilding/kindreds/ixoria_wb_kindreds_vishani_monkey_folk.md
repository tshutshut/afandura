# The Vishani (Monkey Folk)

The Vishani, known elsewhere as monkey folk, are the Kindred of the arts. Where the Keel are drawn to knowledge and the Amdir to building, the Vishani devote themselves to music, theater, dance, painting, poetry and every other form through which something may be expressed. They have a homeland of their own in the south, yet they are found throughout Ixoria, in its cities, its great performance halls and the households of those wealthy enough to commission their work. Few peoples are as admired for their talent, and few are as difficult to live beside.

To an outsider, a Vishani performance is a work of art. To the Vishani, it is something more. Their art is a form of worship, addressed to an audience that no one else can see, and it carries the weight of a tragedy that shaped their people long before any of them were born.

## Ilaro and Aloro

The ancestral homeland of the Vishani lies in the far northeast of Ixoria, upon two islands facing one another across a narrow strait. In their own tongue, they are called Ilaro and Aloro, the Call and the Answer. The islands were once covered in forest, and the Vishani lived among its trees for generations, long before the rise of the Aurelate. Their art was already a central part of their lives in those days, though the oldest accounts suggest it carried less of the solemnity it carries now.

Today, the islands are empty. The forest that once covered them is gone, and those who have seen them describe grey and barren land, scattered with the remnants of a people who no longer live there. The Vishani have not forgotten them. Ilaro and Aloro remain the homeland they lost, and the place to which they hope one day to return.

## Talimé

During the reign of Lyra the Steadfast, the second Beacon, the Vishani undertook the greatest work in their history. They felled the oldest trees of their forest, trees that had stood for longer than any of them could remember, and from their wood they raised a stage. It was said to be the most beautiful thing their people had ever made, and they came to regard it almost as a person. They named it Talimé, the beloved.

The following season, a blight came to the forest. It spread from tree to tree, and nothing the Vishani attempted could stop it. Where the blight had passed, a long drought followed, and what little the sickness had spared, the drought finished. Within a few years, the islands could no longer sustain the people who lived on them. Whether the felling of the trees truly brought the blight upon them, no one can say. The Vishani, however, have never doubted it. They understood the tragedy as a punishment for what they had taken from the land, and that understanding has shaped everything they have become since.

Talimé still stands upon the abandoned islands. It is grey now, like the forest around it, and it has long been forgotten by all but the Vishani, who do not speak of it lightly. They loved it enough to give it a name, and they have never stopped calling it by that name, even though it cost them everything.

## The Leaving

The Vishani left Ilaro and Aloro on a date that is still remembered. They traveled south to another island, where they settled and where their homeland has remained ever since. They could carry almost nothing with them. What they brought was what they knew by heart: their songs, their stories, their plays and their dances. These became the foundation of everything the Vishani would later build, and the means by which they preserved the memory of a home they could no longer see.

At the time of their migration, the Aurelate had not yet reached the far northeast, and the Vishani were under the rule of no Beacon. When the empire later expanded to their new lands, they welcomed the Beacon Dynasty willingly and without real conflict. In return, they were permitted to govern their own affairs, and their homeland has remained largely autonomous ever since, much as the Ear has under successive powers.

## Umaresh, the Hush House

The Vishani believe that their every performance is witnessed. They do not address their art to a god, nor to any single being, but to a vast and unseen audience they call Umaresh, known more commonly as the Hush House. Its members are never seen and never heard, yet they are believed to be present at every performance, in every place and at every moment, so that a hundred Vishani performing in a hundred different cities may all be certain that they are being watched.

What Umaresh is, the Vishani do not claim to know. Some speak of it as the land that punished them, others as the spirits of the forest they destroyed, and others still as something older, which they offended without understanding. They agree only on what it requires. A performance offered to the Hush House must be flawless, for an error is not merely a failure of art; it is a failure before an audience that never forgets. Their art is therefore an act of devotion, and through a lifetime of flawless performances, the Vishani hope to atone for the wrong their ancestors committed.

This devotion extends far beyond the stage. The Vishani hold that anyone who serves the arts serves Umaresh: the makers of instruments and costumes, the painters of sets, the copyists of scores, and those who tend the vines of Talivé. Work done far from any audience is an offering all the same, for nothing is hidden from the Hush House. Among the Vishani it is often said that there is no backstage for the Hush House.

## Talivé

When the Vishani had settled in the south, they built a place of repentance. They called it Talivé, which means what we return, and it was conceived as an answer to Talimé. Where Talimé had been raised from the oldest trees of their forest, Talivé was built entirely of stone, and not a single tree was felled for its construction. It is a temple and an auditorium at once, vast enough to hold a great part of the Vishani people, yet deliberately simple in its form. Its builders sought grandeur without vanity, and they made no attempt to impress through ornament what they had once tried to achieve by taking from the land.

They also left it open to the living world. Whenever a vine took hold of its walls or a flower began to grow from its stones, the Vishani did not remove it. They tended it instead, and over the generations, Talivé has become green with climbing plants, mosses and flowers, so that the stone their ancestors raised now lies half-hidden beneath the life they allowed to return. To the Vishani, this is no accident. Talimé was made by taking from the forest, while Talivé grows by giving back to it.

Talivé is also the greatest school of the arts in Ixoria. Every form of art is taught within its walls, and every one of them is taught as a sacred discipline as much as a craft. Students learn not only how to sing, dance, paint or perform, but why it must be done, and for whom.

## The Daily Offering

Every Vishani household begins its day with an offering. The form it takes belongs to each household, and these traditions are passed down through families for generations. One household may sing a short poem before the day begins, while another performs a slow and deliberate dance, almost a form of meditation. Elsewhere, a sketch is drawn in a notebook kept for that purpose alone, or a tune is simply hummed together. However modest, the offering is never neglected, for the Hush House is present in the smallest house as surely as in Talivé itself.

Because the audience is always present, the Vishani regard much of their ordinary life as a kind of performance. They speak with care, move with deliberate grace and conduct themselves as though they were always being watched, which, in their belief, they are. Other Kindreds often admire their elegance, though many find their manner exaggerated and their constant composure insufferable. The Vishani are rarely troubled by such opinions.

Their reverence for the land shapes daily life in other ways. A Vishani will not fell a living tree. Their houses are built of stone and surrounded by tended gardens, and the wood used for their instruments must come from trees that have fallen of their own accord.

They also never applaud. Silence is the only fitting answer to a performance offered to the Hush House, which itself makes no sound. Applause, cheering and the tossing of coins belong to taverns and street corners, and the Vishani regard them as beneath the dignity of true art.

## The Umarai and the Council

The homeland of the Vishani is governed by a council of eighty-eight Umarai, known in common speech as the Hushkeepers, which means those who belong to Umaresh. They are the priests and priestesses of the Vishani, and at the same time their greatest artists. No one is born to the title, and no one may claim it. A Vishani becomes Umarai only after mastering their art, creating works of lasting worth, sharing them widely and earning great respect among their people. The title is held for life, and it can never be taken away.

Because the number of seats is fixed, a new Umarai may only be recognized when a seat falls empty. That recognition belongs to the Umarai themselves, who decide together which Vishani has earned the title. Many accomplished artists wait for years, and some never receive it at all.

The council has no leader. It speaks, however, through one of its members, chosen by the Umarai each year to act as their voice. In common speech, this role is known as the Prologue, after the figure who steps before the curtain to address the audience on behalf of the whole company. In the Vishani tongue, the Prologue is called the Ellah. The Ellah speaks for the council, but holds no authority over the other Umarai.

The daily work of governing is carried out by helpers drawn from the people. Every Vishani of serving age has their name entered among those who may be called upon, and each Umarai decides how many helpers they will need for the coming year, according to how the previous year has gone and what they intend to undertake. The names are then drawn at random. The Vishani do not regard this as chance. They believe that Umaresh guides the drawing, and to be chosen is therefore considered a duty given by the Hush House itself, rather than a position to be sought.

All three choices are made at the same moment each year, at the end of the Unoffering. The helpers are drawn, the Ellah is chosen, and any new Umarai are recognized, so that the government of the Vishani is renewed on the eve of the Return.

## The Unoffering and the Return

Once each year, on the anniversary of the day their ancestors left Ilaro and Aloro, the Vishani enter the Unoffering. For twenty-two days, no art of any kind is performed. No household makes its daily offering, the stages of Talivé remain empty, and the great performance halls of Ixoria fall silent wherever the Vishani are found. Throughout these days, the Vishani fast from dawn to dusk and live as frugally as they can, gathering each evening for plain meals shared among their community. The Unoffering is a time of remembrance and humility, and the Vishani regard it as the moment in which their whole year is set right again.

During the Unoffering, the Vishani set aside their usual finery and dress in the colors of ash, in memory of the homeland that withered. The shade of these garments darkens with age. Children wear white or the palest grey, the grey deepens over the course of a life, and the eldest of each community wear black. For a people who spend the rest of the year in the eyes of an audience, appearing so plainly is itself a sacrifice.

The Unoffering ends with Ishalem, known in common speech as the Return. It is the greatest performance of the Vishani year, held in Talivé for those in the homeland and in the great performance halls of other cities for those who live elsewhere. It is also the only performance at which the Vishani allow themselves to applaud. The restraint of the whole year gives way in a single night, and the Return is known throughout Ixoria for its excess. Audiences cheer, dance in the aisles and shower the stage with coins, gold and, among the wealthiest, even jewelry. Everything gathered that night is given to the performance house that held it, to sustain it through the year to come.

The name of the performance carries the hope of the whole people. The Vishani believe that a lifetime of flawless offerings may one day atone for what their ancestors did, and that the Hush House may then allow them to return to Ilaro and Aloro. Each year, as the Unoffering ends, they celebrate not only the return of their art, but the return they still hope to see.

## The Vishani and the Ilie

Of all the peoples of Ixoria, the Ilie are perhaps the ones the Vishani regard with the greatest disapproval. The two are often found in the same places, for both are drawn to the stage, yet they could hardly approach it more differently. For the Ilie, performing is mainly a source of joy and amusement, and even when it carries deep meaning, it carries no religious belief. For the Vishani, every performance is an offering to the Hush House, and the Ilie's ease on the stage, their applause and their taverns seem a profanation of something sacred.

The deeper discomfort lies elsewhere. The Ilie are known for laughing at misfortune, and for making a celebration of it. To the Vishani, whose entire history has been shaped by a misfortune they understood as punishment, this borders on blasphemy. Yet the Ilie, who often possess very little, have made something rich and alive out of their own losses, while the Vishani continue to carry theirs through every performance of their lives. Whether this is what the Vishani truly resent, few of them would ever admit.
