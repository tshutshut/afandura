The Ilie

The Ilie are found throughout Ixoria Mora, though rarely among those who hold wealth, influence, or inherited power. They have no kingdom of their own and no ancestral homeland to which they can point. They are most commonly found in cities, particularly in crowded districts where strangers attract less attention and work is plentiful, though Ilie can be found in ports, market towns, villages, and along the roads that connect them. Some remain in one place for most of their lives, becoming familiar figures within a neighborhood, while others travel frequently according to the work available to them. Their lives are generally modest, and they are seldom found among the upper strata of society, but they are not a people apart from it. They work, trade, perform, marry, raise families, and settle wherever circumstances allow.

What makes the Ilie distinct is not where they come from, but what happens to them. An ordinary person may live an entirely ordinary life and, without warning, become Ilie. There is no known cause for the transformation, nor has anyone found a reliable pattern in those who undergo it. A person may go to sleep as they have always been and awaken changed. The transformation is permanent, and no known case exists of an Ilie returning to their former state.

The Transformation

The change affects the body in ways that have given the Ilie their reputation throughout the realm. Horns may grow from the head, a tail may appear, the skin may change in color or texture, and the eyes may take on unfamiliar forms. Some transformations are subtle, while others leave little doubt that the person who emerged from sleep is no longer quite the person their family knew the day before. Yet there is no single appearance shared by all Ilie. Even among those who possess similar features, the details differ, and stranger changes are known to occur.

The transformation offers no indication of the person beneath it. A kind person may awaken with an appearance that frightens those around them, while someone of poor character may undergo a change that leaves them almost untouched in appearance. The Ilie have therefore learned, more than most, that appearance is a poor measure of character. Whatever force causes the transformation seems to make no distinction between virtue and vice, wealth and poverty, youth and age.

Almost any person may become Ilie. Humans, Elves, Dwarves, Halflings, Gnomes, Half-Elves, Half-Orcs, Aevra, and other peoples of Ixoria Mora have all been known to undergo the transformation. Beastkin are the sole exception. No Beastkin has ever been known to become Ilie, though whether this is a natural boundary or merely an absence of recorded cases remains unknown.

The Burden of Becoming

For many, the transformation is as difficult for those around them as it is for the person who undergoes it. Families may regard the change as a curse, a punishment, or an omen of some misfortune yet to come. In places where old beliefs remain strong, the appearance of an Ilie within a household may be treated as a sign that the household itself has been marked. Some families accept their transformed kin, but others do not, and an Ilie may find themselves pushed from the home they once considered permanent.

Some leave willingly. They fear that their presence will bring shame or hardship upon those they love, or simply find that the life they once knew can no longer be recovered. Others are given no choice. The road therefore becomes familiar to many Ilie, particularly during the first years after their transformation, when they must find new work, new companions, and sometimes an entirely new place in the world.

Yet becoming Ilie does not condemn a person to wandering. Cities in particular have long provided places where they can establish themselves without entirely escaping the prejudices of those around them. An Ilie may settle above a tavern, share a room with other travelers, rent a small house, or build a life around whatever work they have found. Over time, they may become as familiar to a street or district as anyone else, even if they remain unlikely to rise far within the society around them.

The Changed Voice

The transformation may alter more than appearance. Some Ilie find that their voice changes after becoming Ilie, while others develop an unusual ability to reproduce sounds they have heard. A skilled Ilie may imitate another person's voice closely enough to deceive an unfamiliar listener, reproduce the calls of animals, or recreate sounds from memory. The ability differs from one person to another, much as their physical transformations do.

Among the Ilie, this is simply another of the mysteries that accompanied their change. Elsewhere, it has become one of the reasons they are sought after as performers. A voice that can become another voice is useful on a stage, and an Ilie with a talent for imitation may find work where others would find only suspicion.

Music and theatre consequently hold a particular place in Ilian life. Not every Ilie is an entertainer, and not every entertainer is Ilie, but the two have become closely associated in many cities. For those who make their living this way, the same strange voice that once marked them as different can become the means by which they earn a living.

The Second Name

Among the Ilie, a transformed person may eventually receive a second name. It is not chosen by the person who receives it, but given by other Ilie. The custom has no single ceremony observed throughout the realm. In some communities the name emerges after a period of travel, while elsewhere it may be given following an event, a habit, a particular manner of speaking, or a story that has come to define the person in the eyes of those around them.

The name is more than a nickname. It marks a new place within the Ilian community. A person who has lost the name and life they were born into is given something that comes from those who understand what they have become. The name is a recognition that, whatever has been lost, they are no longer alone in it.

Devil's Luck

The Ilie have a reputation for finding trouble and, with equal regularity, escaping it.

An Ilie may take the wrong road, anger the wrong official, become involved in a dispute that was never theirs, lose their belongings, or find themselves in circumstances from which a sensible person should have little hope of escape. Yet stories of Ilian misfortune rarely end where they ought to. The stolen purse is recovered by accident. The angry magistrate turns out to need a musician. The broken wagon arrives at its destination anyway. The person who seemed certain to be arrested somehow walks away with a job.

The Ilie call this Devil's Luck. Whether it is truly luck, coincidence, or merely the result of Ilian stubbornness and adaptability has never been settled. The stories themselves matter more than the explanation, and they are told often enough that almost every Ilie seems to have one of their own.

The Feast of Nothing

At the beginning of autumn, when the first leaves begin to fall and the warmth of the year starts to withdraw, Ilie gather wherever enough of them happen to be within reach of one another. There is no sacred city to which they must travel and no single place where the feast is held. In one year it may take place in the back room of a tavern; in another, beneath the awnings of a market; elsewhere it may be held around a collection of fires outside a city. The gathering belongs to those who attend it, and its size depends entirely upon who happens to be there.

The season itself has long carried meaning for the Ilie. Autumn is the time when the world begins to let go of what it cannot carry into winter. Leaves fall, fields surrender their final harvests, and travelers begin to prepare for colder roads. The Ilie know something of leaving things behind. Many have abandoned homes, families, names, or lives that they once believed would last. Yet the tree remains when its leaves are gone, and the road continues after the traveler has passed. The turning of the season is therefore a reminder that loss does not necessarily mean an ending.

The feast takes its name from an old Ilian custom: everyone may bring something, but nobody is required to bring anything. Those with food bring what they can spare. A loaf of bread, a pot of stew, a few apples, cheese, preserved meat, wine, or whatever else has found its way into their possession is placed among the common meal. Those who have nothing are expected to sit and eat all the same. Nothing is considered a sufficient contribution.

As the meal continues, the gathering becomes louder and more cheerful. Musicians play, voices imitate one another across the tables, and stories of Lucky Misfortune are exchanged. These are not tales of great victories, but accounts of foolish decisions, unfortunate accidents, bad luck, and situations that should have ended terribly. The best stories are those in which everything goes wrong before somehow turning in the storyteller's favor. Friends interrupt to correct old details, strangers add their own versions, and stories that began as ordinary mishaps become part of the shared memory of the community.

Those who have recently become Ilie and have not yet received their second name may also receive it during the feast. There is no priest, official, or elder appointed to perform the naming. Instead, the people gathered around the table speak of the person, recall what they have seen of them, and eventually a name emerges. It may come from a deed, a habit, an appearance, a voice, or some small incident that others have already begun to remember. The new Ilie does not choose the name. They receive it from those who have already learned to see them as one of their own.

The Feast of Nothing is therefore both a meal and a remembrance. It is a gathering of people who possess little in common except the strange circumstance that brought them together. They bring what they have, laugh at what went wrong, give names to those who have yet to receive them, and make room for whoever arrives at the table.

By the following morning, the gathering begins to disperse. Some return to the rooms and streets where they live, some take the road again, and others remain behind to finish another season where they have settled. The feast does not promise that their lives will become easier. It only reminds them, for a little while, that whatever the road may take from them, there will always be another Ilie willing to make room at the table.
