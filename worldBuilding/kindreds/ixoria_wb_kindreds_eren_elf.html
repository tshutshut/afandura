The Eren (Elf)

The Eren, known elsewhere as elves, are the longest-lived people of Ixoria. They possess no realm of their own and claim no distant woodland kingdom. They are found throughout the continent, woven into its cities and settlements as artisans, merchants, priests, soldiers, politicians, laborers, scholars, criminals, and almost anything else a person might become when given several centuries in which to try. Longevity has not made the Eren serene. An Eren may live for several hundred years, but age eventually takes its toll: memory becomes unreliable, thoughts lose their clarity, the body weakens, and independence slowly gives way to dependence. The oldest Eren may spend decades cared for by descendants they no longer recognize. For them, longevity is both blessing and burden, the promise of an extraordinarily long life accompanied by the knowledge that its final chapter may be one of gradual decline.

Perhaps because of this, younger Eren have acquired a reputation for recklessness. They travel, experiment, break conventions, change professions, enter dangerous trades, and sometimes involve themselves in the less respectable corners of society. Centuries of potential life do not always make them cautious; quite often, they have the opposite effect. The Eren know what waits at the far end of their years, and many seem determined to live fiercely while those years still belong entirely to them. Yet however far an Eren wanders, family has a way of drawing them home.

The Eren Household

Eren families commonly gather into close-knit urban communities. Their traditional homes are built vertically around a central open courtyard, with several branches of an extended family inhabiting the same building. A humble household may possess little more than a narrow court surrounded by crowded rooms and wooden galleries. Wealthier lineages may have fountains, gardens and trees beneath broad balconies, while the most prosperous can occupy several connected mansions arranged around extensive grounds. The scale changes, but the principle remains: an Eren household turns inward toward its family.

The courtyard is its living heart, where children play, meals spill into the open air, relatives meet beneath the sky, and several generations encounter one another simply by going about their day. The ground floor contains the household's most communal and accessible spaces. A large kitchen feeds the many branches of the family, while a reception hall allows outsiders to be welcomed without immediately entering its more intimate spaces. Being invited beyond that hall and into the family courtyard is itself a small gesture of trust.

The oldest Eren also live on the ground floor. Their rooms are comfortable, accessible and deliberately kept close to the ordinary life of the household. Decline is not something the Eren hide away; an elder who can no longer climb stairs or remember the names of their descendants still hears children running through the courtyard and smells meals being prepared nearby. Above them live the younger generations, with separate branches occupying different floors and quarters, giving individual families privacy without separating them from the greater household. At the very top lie the family archives. There is an old principle behind this arrangement: the older and more vulnerable the person, the closer to the ground they live; the older and more precious the memory, the higher it is kept.

The Family Memory

Every established Eren family keeps a written record of itself. For a young lineage, this may consist of only a handful of volumes. For families whose histories stretch back centuries, shelves become rooms, rooms become libraries, and generations of handwriting accumulate into an extraordinary record of lives otherwise long forgotten. These are not merely genealogies. Births and deaths are recorded beside marriages, journeys, quarrels, achievements, failures, scandals, reconciliations and stories that matter to nobody outside the family. The books preserve both the remarkable and the mundane because the Eren understand that nobody can know which memory will become precious once the person who carried it begins to forget.

Losing one of these volumes is therefore considered a genuine family catastrophe. Fire, theft or destruction can erase something that no living person is capable of reconstructing; a witness may still be alive and yet no longer remember clearly enough to replace what was lost. The archives are consequently among the most heavily protected places in an Eren household. They are traditionally kept on its uppermost floors behind strong doors, with access entrusted to very few. Chief among them is the Memory Keeper.

The Memory Keeper

The Memory Keeper is not necessarily the oldest member of the household and holds no right to rule the family. Their position is one of responsibility rather than authority. A Keeper is usually an older Eren who remains physically capable and mentally clear, someone who has lived long enough to understand the family and its history while still possessing the faculties necessary to preserve it faithfully.

Accepting the position means leaving much of one's previous life behind. The Keeper takes up residence near the archives and assumes responsibility for the family's records, testimonies and secrets. Their service has no fixed duration: one may remain Keeper for five years, another for thirty. They continue until they judge, or those closest to them recognize, that age has begun to compromise their ability to perform the task. Appointment as Memory Keeper is therefore both an honor and a sobering moment. It means that the family trusts an Eren with things it may entrust to nobody else, but it also marks the beginning of a new chapter in that Eren's life, and everyone knows what chapter is likely to follow it.

Remembering Day

The Eren possess no universal date for Remembering Day. Each household or community calls its own gatherings according to its traditions, needs and circumstances. Some may hold them relatively often; others allow years to pass. What matters is that the family returns. Eren who have wandered elsewhere are expected, when reasonably able, to come home, and repeatedly refusing to attend without good cause can diminish an Eren's standing within their family. Remembering Day is not merely a celebration; it is part of the mechanism through which the family remembers itself.

The day has two faces. The first is public. Members of the family gather, usually within the courtyard, and speak about what has happened since the last gathering. Achievements are celebrated, losses mourned, births welcomed, journeys recounted and important changes acknowledged. Invited outsiders may attend this portion, allowing close friends, spouses and companions from other kindreds to become part of an Eren family's shared memory.

The second face belongs only to the individual and the Memory Keeper. Behind closed doors, one Eren at a time may give private testimony concerning things they believe must be preserved but do not wish to declare before the family: a confession, a shame, a secret love, a betrayal, the hidden reason behind some decision, or simply a truth whose importance may not become apparent for decades. Thus an old Eren archive contains more than the history a family tells about itself; it also contains the history that its members could tell only to one person.

The Passing of Memory

A Remembering Day sometimes marks the end of a Keeper's service. Among the great archive volumes is a much smaller book, deliberately plain and almost insignificant beside the histories surrounding it. It contains only names: every Memory Keeper who has served the family, written one beneath another. No accomplishments accompany them, and there are no judgments upon whether they served well or poorly. In an ancient household, those names may stretch backward for centuries.

When an old Keeper can no longer continue, their final act in office is to open this book and write the name of their successor. They never write their own name; that was done long ago by the Keeper who came before them. Before the gathered family, the old Keeper closes the book and places it in the hands of the new, then surrenders the keys to the family archives. There are no crowns and no ceremonial vestments. The book and the keys are enough. The old Keeper says, “I am remembered.” The new Keeper answers, “I will remember.” With that, the exchange is complete. The former Keeper returns to the ordinary life of the household, while the successor moves closer to the archives and assumes responsibility for everything the family has chosen to preserve. Eventually, if they live long enough, they will stand on the other side of the same exchange.

The Last Chapter

The Eren do not fear death in quite the same way that they fear an unfinished life. An elf who dies after several centuries is mourned, certainly, but their death is understood as the natural completion of a long existence. Their experiences have been recorded, their testimony has been given, and their descendants have cared for them through their decline. Their book has reached its final page.

Eren communities maintain cemeteries of their own, particularly around established Eren neighborhoods. Their dead are buried rather than cremated, and their tombstones make an important distinction. An Eren who dies naturally after a long life is marked by the image of a closed book. The symbol does not mean that the dead are forgotten; quite the opposite. Their story has been preserved, and its ending has been reached.

The death of a young Eren is something else entirely. Among a people who expect centuries of life, youth extends far beyond childhood. An Eren may have been an adult for decades and still die terribly young in the eyes of their community. Such a grave is deliberately left unfinished. Stone may remain rough, ornamentation may stop abruptly, and no later generation is permitted to complete what was left incomplete. Upon the tombstone is an open book. The same absence may be preserved within the family archive, where a space is left for the later entries that should have appeared and is never subsequently filled. The Eren have an old expression for such deaths: “An open book for a life unfinished.” For them, few words carry greater sorrow.

The Ilie and the Nyr

Over generations, the most emotionally charged moments of Eren life have brought them unusually close to two other peoples of Ixoria. The Ilie have long been welcomed as performers at Eren gatherings. Their music accompanies celebrations, family events and Remembering Days, but their role becomes especially important in mourning. Where grief leaves an Eren household struggling to give voice to what it feels, Ilie musicians and singers often do so for them. These relationships can survive for generations, with the same Ilie families or traditions returning repeatedly to the same Eren household to perform at marriages, births, gatherings and eventually funerals separated by decades.

The Nyr occupy a quieter place. Their willingness to undertake work that others avoid has long included duties surrounding death. Nyr may prepare bodies, transport the dead and assist with burial, and to an Eren family facing the premature loss of one of its own, such practical acts can become almost impossible to perform. The Nyr do what grief prevents the family from doing. Neither relationship is founded upon formal alliance; both grew instead from repeated contact at moments when the Eren were most vulnerable. The Ilie help them express grief, while the Nyr help them endure what grief demands, and for a people often considered aloof by those outside their courtyards, such relationships are not easily forgotten.
