# WB - Kindreds - Keel

The Keel, known elsewhere as gnomes, are among the most intellectually driven peoples of Ixoria. Their societies place extraordinary value upon learning, investigation, invention and, above all, contribution. Intelligence is admired among them, but intelligence alone is not considered an accomplishment. A Kell may spend decades studying a subject and acquire formidable expertise, yet ultimately be judged by a much more difficult question: what did they do with what they knew?

To contribute does not necessarily mean making a great discovery or creating something that changes Ixoria. A physician who develops a better treatment has contributed. So has an engineer who improves a bridge, an investigator who solves a difficult case, a botanist who understands why a crop is failing, or an adviser who finds a way through a political crisis. The Keel can consequently be found in an enormous variety of professions, provided those professions give them problems to understand and opportunities to solve them.

This also lies behind one of the deepest anxieties within Keel culture. The Keel do not particularly fear being surpassed. Knowledge is expected to progress, and another person improving upon one's work does not invalidate the contribution that came before. What the Keel dread is irrelevance: possessing knowledge but having nothing useful to offer, confronting a problem and finding themselves unable to advance it, or passing through a long life without being able to point toward something that became different because they were there. For a Kell, failure is not necessarily disgraceful. An experiment can fail and teach something, an investigation can follow a false path and eliminate a possibility, and being wrong can itself contribute to understanding. Far worse is to have nothing to add at all. This combination of intellect, ambition and service has shaped Keel society for centuries.

## Life and Longevity

The Keel are long-lived compared with most peoples of Ixoria, although their lives are considerably shorter than those of the Eren. Under favorable circumstances, a Kell may live for a couple of centuries. This gives them considerable time to study, develop expertise and pursue several stages of an intellectual or professional career.

Their longevity reinforces the expectations placed upon them. A Kell has time to learn, but that also means they have time to accomplish something with what they learn. Decades spent studying are not regarded as wasted when they are preparation for later work, but education is understood as preparation rather than contribution in itself. A learned Kell who has accumulated knowledge but never put it to use may therefore feel profoundly dissatisfied regardless of how respected their education might otherwise be.

This does not mean every Kell must become a celebrated scholar. Keel culture makes room for remarkably modest contributions. Improving the irrigation of one village may matter as much to the person who accomplished it as publishing a major work of magical theory. What matters is that a problem existed, the Kell applied their abilities to it, and something changed.

## The Ear

The ancestral homeland of the Keel is the Ear, a peninsula in southwestern Ixoria. Its oldest cities have been inhabited by Keel communities for centuries, alongside smaller towns, villages and rural settlements. Although the Keel are now common far beyond the peninsula, the Ear remains the historical center of their culture and the home of their oldest institutions.

The Ear has never been characterized by territorial ambition. Its communities have traditionally been more concerned with scholarship, research and internal affairs than expansion. Several of its cities maintain strong traditions of local government and independence, and the peninsula as a whole has generally enjoyed an unusual degree of autonomy under the larger powers that have ruled Ixoria.

This was true under the Aurelate and remains broadly true under the Ascendancy. Keel communities have rarely attempted armed resistance merely to reject a distant sovereign, provided that sovereign is willing to leave their institutions substantially undisturbed. They will recognize an emperor, pay taxes, negotiate obligations and accommodate new political realities if doing so allows the Ear to continue its work. In return, successive rulers have had compelling reasons not to interfere excessively. The Ear produces physicians, engineers, investigators, scholars, advisers, naturalists, magical researchers and other specialists whose expertise is valuable throughout Ixoria. Its influence therefore derives less from armies or territory than from the usefulness of its people and institutions.

The arrangement has endured because both sides generally understand what the other receives from it. The Ear does not seek to rule its neighbors, and rulers who leave it largely to its own affairs gain access to generations of accumulated expertise.

## Leaving the Ear

Although the Ear is the ancestral home of Keel civilization, the Keel are particularly common in the great cities of Ixoria. Their presence elsewhere is not the consequence of exile or the decline of their homeland. Quite the opposite: leaving the Ear has become a natural extension of the values taught there.

A Kell can learn an enormous amount on the peninsula. Its libraries, schools, workshops and institutions provide opportunities that are difficult to reproduce elsewhere. Eventually, however, many begin looking outward. Knowledge is meant to be used, and a newly trained physician may travel somewhere where physicians are scarce, an engineer may enter the service of a city struggling with sanitation or flooding, an investigator may seek difficult cases in a major capital, a botanist might travel to unfamiliar climates, while a magical researcher may join an institution studying phenomena unavailable on the Ear. Others become royal advisers, administrators, teachers, architects, mathematicians or consultants.

The nature of those they serve matters relatively little. Keel intellectual culture does not teach that knowledge exists solely for the Keel. Expertise gains value by being put to use, regardless of whether those benefiting from it are Keel, humans, Eren, Ilie or any other people. There is undeniably a self-interested element to this tradition: service gives a Kell the opportunity to prove that they have something to contribute. Yet that desire for personal relevance has produced a culture in which intellectual achievement and service to others frequently reinforce one another. A Kell wants to matter, and one of the clearest ways to matter is to become useful to someone who needs what they know.

## Orden College

At the center of intellectual life on the Ear stands Orden College, the peninsula's most prestigious institution of higher learning and one of the most respected centers of scholarship in Ixoria. Orden is not restricted to the Keel. Students from other kindreds may study there, although admission is demanding and the standards expected after admission are equally severe. Its reputation rests not upon exclusivity by ancestry but upon the quality of the work produced within it.

The College encompasses a remarkable variety of disciplines. Medicine, engineering, mathematics, magical theory, botany, zoology and other natural sciences coexist alongside law, investigation and fields concerned with administration and practical problem-solving. New areas of study have emerged over the centuries as knowledge has developed.

Orden was created through the cooperation of four Kell scholars who already maintained their own schools, workshops or circles of students. They were Orda Venn, Tobran Malk, Selki Marr and Pell Orro. Orda Venn studied medicine and the living sciences. Her contribution to the founding of the College was particularly tangible: she donated the acres surrounding her own institution so that the four schools could be brought together on one site. Buildings rose around her original school, collections and libraries were gradually combined, and the institution that emerged eventually took the name Orden, derived from Orda herself.

Tobran Malk was an engineer concerned with applied mechanics and practical solutions. Bridges, irrigation, sanitation, mechanisms and construction belonged naturally to his world. Selki Marr became associated with investigation and evidence, developing approaches to witnesses, observation, reconstruction and the distinction between assumption and demonstrable fact. Pell Orro was a mathematician and magical theorist whose interests extended into logic, abstraction and areas of scholarship for which no immediate practical application could necessarily be demonstrated.

## Elenna the Enlightened and the Founding of Orden

The four schools were brought together during the reign of Elenna the Enlightened, at a time when the long conflict later remembered through the Stonewall Campaign remained unresolved. While traveling through the southwest near the Ear, Elenna's party was unexpectedly ambushed and the Beacon herself was wounded by a poisoned weapon. Her attendants and physicians succeeded in stabilizing her, but they could not understand why her condition continued to deteriorate. The attack had taken place close enough to Orda Venn's school that her expertise could be sought quickly. Orda recognized that the wound itself was no longer the principal danger and adapted Elenna's treatment to the poison introduced through it. The Beacon survived, but her recovery required her to remain among the Keel for several weeks.

The political consequences of the attack threatened to become much larger than the ambush itself. The Aurelate was already in conflict with the Orcish territories, and many immediately assumed that the Orcs had attempted to assassinate the reigning Beacon. Voices favoring a stronger military campaign seized upon the attack as further justification for escalation. Selki Marr, however, was troubled by the explanation from the beginning. The Ear lay far from the Orcish homeland and from the principal territories in dispute, and an Orcish strike so deep into the southwest did not fit what she understood of the conflict. Orda, who had long enjoyed a particularly close relationship with Selki, brought her into the matter. Contemporary accounts make their mutual trust clear, although later generations have never settled whether the bond between the two women extended beyond friendship and collegial affection.

Selki investigated without accepting the prevailing assumption. She examined the site of the attack, separated testimony from inference, compared the accounts of survivors and reconstructed what could be established from the available evidence. Her work ultimately demonstrated that the attackers were not Orcs at all, but members of an emerging brigand faction seeking notoriety. Through preparation, opportunism and considerable luck, they had managed to wound the Beacon. Selki's conclusion not only identified the culprits but prevented a false accusation from becoming the justification for a major escalation of the Stonewall conflict.

Elenna's prolonged recovery gave her an unusual opportunity to observe the intellectual life of the Ear directly. Through Orda and Selki she became acquainted with Pell Orro, Tobran Malk and the separate schools and circles maintained by the four scholars. Elenna encouraged them to consider what their work might become if their efforts were brought together. The decision remained theirs. Orda offered the land surrounding her own institution, the four schools began combining their students, libraries, workshops and collections, and Orden College emerged from their cooperation.

Elenna supported the new College with substantial imperial patronage, helping finance its construction and expansion while leaving its scholarship and internal affairs in Keel hands. Her experience on the Ear had demonstrated something more important than the simple usefulness of Keel learning: Orda had saved her life through independent expertise, while Selki had challenged an explanation favored by powerful voices within the Aurelate and prevented a potentially disastrous decision. The autonomy granted to Orden became part of a broader precedent for the unusual freedom enjoyed by the Ear under the Aurelate. The Keel did not seek territorial power, while the empire benefited enormously from their physicians, engineers, investigators, scholars and advisers. The arrangement endured long beyond Elenna's reign and helped shape the relationship later powers maintained with the peninsula.

Their original traditions survive in the four Houses of Orden: Venn, Malk, Marr and Orro. The Houses are not faculties. A physician need not belong to Venn, nor an engineer to Malk. Students belonging to any House may study within any faculty. Centuries of institutional development have separated the social traditions of the Houses from the academic organization of the College. Nevertheless, House membership continues to matter. Each has inherited methods, traditions, networks and forms of training originating in its founder's work. These distinctions may eventually have practical consequences for Kell characters belonging to one of the Houses, although they do not determine personality or restrict a character's profession.

Two of Orden's founders are remembered for something considerably less dignified than their academic achievements.

## Pell Orro and Tobran Malk

During the early years of Orden College, Pell Orro and Tobran Malk shared an office, and they did not enjoy the arrangement. Their intellectual temperaments were almost perfectly designed to irritate one another. Tobran was an engineer whose instincts pulled relentlessly toward practical application. A problem was something to be solved, and the worth of an idea was often clearest when something could be built, repaired, improved or made more efficient because of it. Pell was perfectly willing to devote months to mathematics, logic or magical theory without knowing whether his conclusions would ever produce an immediate practical result.

Their habits irritated one another just as much as their scholarship did. Tobran, in particular, was known for his hats. He owned many of them, and although the hats themselves were generally distinguished rather than outrageous, he enjoyed decorating them with feathers. Some were ordinary feathers, while others came from uncommon birds and could be surprisingly expensive.

During one of their frequent arguments, Tobran criticized Pell's research for producing nothing of practical value. He questioned how much time, ink and College resources Pell intended to consume before his work actually solved something. Pell was offended, but he did not respond immediately. Instead, he began studying Tobran.

For months, Pell quietly documented the hats his office mate wore. He catalogued the feathers, attempted to identify the birds from which they came, recorded how often particular feathers appeared, consulted merchants about their prices and estimated how much Tobran was spending on ornamental plumage. Tradition maintains that Pell even interviewed Tobran's hatmaker. Eventually he announced a formal presentation.

### An Inquiry into the Economic Consequences of Tobran Malk's Continuing Persecution of the Birds of Ixoria

Pell delivered the presentation with complete academic seriousness. He began by cataloguing what he described as Tobran's victims. Illustrations identified the relevant birds and the feathers that had appeared on particular hats. Pell estimated the financial cost of Tobran's collection and, for rhetorical effect, attempted to calculate how many birds had suffered to maintain it. His research had uncovered an inconvenient fact: several of the birds naturally shed the feathers Tobran wore. Pell acknowledged this, then dismissed it as detrimental to his argument.

From there, the presentation turned to chickens. Pell compared the price of Tobran's preferred ornamental feathers with the cost of several common breeds of chicken available on the Ear. In some cases, he demonstrated that Tobran could purchase an entire chicken for less than the price of a single decorative feather. The chicken possessed an additional advantage: it produced more feathers, and it also produced eggs.

Pell therefore calculated the expected egg production of the various breeds, estimated the income Tobran could obtain by selling surplus eggs, calculated how many additional chickens could subsequently be purchased with those profits and projected the expansion of Tobran's hypothetical poultry enterprise over several years. Finally, he calculated how much legitimate academic research could have been financed using the resulting income. Pell's conclusion was that Tobran's choice of expensive feathers was actively obstructing the advancement of Keel knowledge.

The audience did not survive the presentation with its dignity intact. Pell maintained his seriousness for an impressive amount of time while students and scholars increasingly lost their composure. Tobran eventually began laughing as well. Pell followed shortly afterward, and the lecture dissolved. Tobran did not retaliate immediately. He waited almost exactly a year.

## The Egg-Peeling Machine

Sharing an office had given Tobran plenty of material of his own. Pell regularly ate a boiled egg while working. He would peel it at his desk, dispose of the shell and continue with his morning. It was an utterly ordinary habit. Tobran decided that it represented a crisis.

On the anniversary of Pell's presentation, Tobran announced his own lecture:

### The Appalling Inefficiency of Pell Orro's Consumption of Eggs and a Mechanical Remedy Thereof

Tobran had spent months observing and timing Pell's breakfast. He calculated how many seconds Pell required to peel an egg, how often fragments of shell remained attached, how much additional time was spent removing them, how long it took to dispose of the shell and clean his fingers, and how frequently fragments had to be removed from papers or the desk. The losses were minuscule. Tobran multiplied them across Pell's projected remaining lifespan and solemnly announced that Pell Orro was destined to waste a measurable portion of his life peeling eggs.

Fortunately, Tobran had engineered a solution. The resulting egg-peeling machine was enormous compared with the problem it addressed. An egg entered at one end and passed through a needlessly complicated sequence of gears, levers, counterweights, brushes, blades and other mechanisms. The shell was cracked, removed and deposited into a separate receptacle before the peeled egg emerged. The machine took longer to peel an egg than Pell did.

Tobran treated this as a minor developmental difficulty. He presented calculations demonstrating how sufficient additional research, funding and several generations of engineering improvements might eventually make the device marginally more efficient than peeling an egg by hand. He then produced the projected cost of this research. It closely resembled the amount Pell had claimed could be generated through Tobran's hypothetical chicken enterprise.

Tobran had one final result to present. He had followed Pell's advice. He had bought a chicken. At the end of the lecture, Tobran produced one of its eggs, walked to Pell and placed it in his hand.

> “Your first dividend.”

Pell was left holding the egg and asked how he was supposed to transport it. Tobran removed one of his feathered hats, placed the egg inside it and handed the hat to Pell. Whatever composure remained in the room disappeared.

So did much of the hostility between the two men. The rivalry did not end; Pell and Tobran continued arguing for the rest of their lives. What changed was the nature of those arguments. The two office mates who had once struggled to tolerate one another became exceptionally close friends and remained so until their deaths. Their absurd exchange also produced one of the most beloved traditions in Keel culture.

## Folly Dolly

Students remembered the two lectures. The following years brought imitations. Students produced deliberately useless research, faculty joined them, elaborate solutions were devised for problems that did not exist, committees appeared to judge the results, rules accumulated, and what began as a private feud between two founders became an annual tradition of Orden College. At some point, it acquired the appropriately foolish name Folly Dolly.

The tradition eventually escaped Orden altogether. Today, once each year, Keel communities and institutions throughout Ixoria participate in Folly Dolly. For one day, the ordinary expectations of Keel intellectual culture are reversed. Intelligence is not supposed to produce anything useful. Scholars, engineers, investigators, students, artisans and children compete to create, discover or demonstrate things of magnificent uselessness.

An engineer might build an unnecessarily complicated device for moving a spoon across a table. A botanist might devote months to producing a plant with an amusing shape. An investigator might reconstruct an entirely meaningless mystery. A mathematician might develop an extraordinarily sophisticated solution to a problem that could have been answered by counting on one's fingers. The central principle is simple: the contribution should accomplish as little as possible while demonstrating as much ingenuity as possible.

Judging is conducted with exaggerated seriousness. Committees scrutinize entries, demand explanations and debate their merits with all the solemnity of a genuine academic examination. A creation that unexpectedly proves genuinely useful risks undermining its own claim to distinction. The winner receives the traditional prize: a hat decorated with a chicken feather and filled with eggs. The ceremony is treated with absurd dignity, after which everyone celebrates. The following morning, the Keel go back to work.

Beneath the comedy lies something important. Keel society places immense pressure upon individuals to contribute, solve and improve. Folly Dolly provides one day during which that expectation is deliberately suspended. A Kell may use their intelligence for absolutely nothing and be celebrated for doing so. The tradition is therefore both ridiculous and, paradoxically, culturally necessary.

## The Folly Relics of Orden

Orden College has never forgotten where Folly Dolly began. Pell Orro's original manuscript, *An Inquiry into the Economic Consequences of Tobran Malk's Continuing Persecution of the Birds of Ixoria*, remains preserved in the College, together with his notes, calculations, feather prices, comparisons of chicken breeds and projections concerning the hypothetical poultry enterprise.

Tobran Malk's response is preserved alongside it. The College retains *The Appalling Inefficiency of Pell Orro's Consumption of Eggs and a Mechanical Remedy Thereof*, including Tobran's observations of Pell's breakfast habits, calculations concerning the accumulated time lost to egg peeling, plans for the machine and estimates for its continued development.

The machine itself also survives. It has not functioned for centuries. Age has damaged its mechanisms, and some of its components can no longer move as intended. Orden possesses both the expertise and the original plans necessary to attempt a restoration, but the machine remains as it is: an elaborate, broken solution to a problem that never required solving.

Together, the papers and machine are among the strangest historical treasures of one of Ixoria's most prestigious academic institutions. For all the discoveries associated with Orden College, generations of Keel remain particularly fond of the story of two brilliant scholars, forced to share an office, who disliked one another enough to spend nearly two years producing rigorous academic arguments about feathered hats and boiled eggs—and who emerged from the experience as lifelong friends.
