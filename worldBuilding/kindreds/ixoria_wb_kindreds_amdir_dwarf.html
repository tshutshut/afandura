Amdir

The Kindred of Lasting Works

The Amdir, commonly known throughout Ixoria as dwarves, are a long-lived kindred whose identity is closely bound to what they create. A road that serves generations, a bridge that endures centuries of weather, or a hall that shelters a community is more than a useful structure. It is a lasting record of those who conceived it, built it, and accepted responsibility for its endurance. Their reputation rests not merely upon technical skill but upon the reliability of works entrusted with other people's lives.

Amdir builders have traveled widely, opening mountain passages, constructing harbors, raising fortifications, and undertaking commissions far beyond their own settlements. They commonly live between two and three centuries, long enough to revisit works completed in youth and judge how well they have endured. Their regard for permanence does not mean that buildings must remain unchanged. Repairs and alterations may be left visible, preserving the history of a structure and acknowledging the care taken to keep it standing. Maintaining an old crossing can carry as much dignity as building a new one.

The Amdir are willing to share their labor with other peoples, though they are more guarded with the methods and knowledge accumulated through generations of practice. Such knowledge belongs not simply to individual craftsmen but to the communities that preserved it. Their creations may stand throughout Ixoria, yet the traditions behind them remain deeply rooted in Amdir society.

Apprentices and Mentors

Nearly every Amdir begins as an apprentice. The bond between apprentice and mentor extends well beyond instruction in tools, materials, and techniques, often becoming one of the most important relationships in an Amdir's life. An apprentice follows their mentor not only to workshops and construction sites but also to markets, gatherings, visits, and meetings seemingly unrelated to the craft. To outsiders, the sight of a younger Amdir accompanying an elder almost everywhere is entirely ordinary.

Through this closeness, apprentices learn how their mentors judge a difficult problem, negotiate a commission, settle disagreements, and conduct themselves toward others. They acquire habits and values alongside technical knowledge, making the identity of one's mentor a measure of character as well as professional lineage. These relationships frequently grow into lifelong friendships or bonds resembling family, and an apprentice's independence need not bring the relationship to an end.

Not every accomplished craftsman becomes a mentor. Teaching requires patience, judgment, and a willingness to accept responsibility for another person's development. Amdir society recognizes that mastery of a craft does not automatically confer the ability to guide someone through it.

The Guilds of Creation

The guilds are the principal institutions of Amdir life, organized not around individual professions but around the things their members create. A guild devoted to bridges may include surveyors, designers, stoneworkers, carpenters, and metalworkers. Their different skills serve a common work, while the guild preserves the techniques, histories, and reputations accumulated through successive generations of apprentices and mentors. An Amdir may also pursue a new field later in life, sometimes accepting another apprenticeship to do so.

Among the oldest and most renowned is the Guild of Ways, responsible for roads, bridges, crossings, and the physical connections between settlements. Its engineers once held exceptional prestige, for a difficult road or bridge could change the fortunes of an entire region. The Guild of Pillars, by contrast, is devoted to works that give a city or realm visible form: monumental halls, ceremonial structures, public squares, and imposing administrative buildings. Where the Guild of Ways connects places, the Guild of Pillars shapes how those places present themselves to the world.

Other guilds serve different kinds of construction, but these two have become the most politically consequential. Their fortunes rose and fell according to what Ixoria required, and their rivalry now reflects a disagreement over which works deserve the greatest authority within Amdir society.

The Five Circles

Amdir society recognizes five successive circles of responsibility. The first is that of the apprentice, who learns a craft and the judgment expected of its practitioners. The second belongs to the craftsman, recognized as capable of undertaking work independently and bearing responsibility for its outcome. The third belongs to the guild elder, whose experience and standing allow them to preserve traditions, guide younger members, and influence the affairs of their guild. Many Amdir remain elders throughout their lives, preferring their craft to political office.

Those who enter government may become councilors in the Forum, the broader deliberative institution of Amdir society. The fifth and innermost circle is the Concord, whose members bear the title of grand councilor. The prominent guilds are represented there, and together these institutions exercise collective authority without a hereditary monarch or a single ruler standing above them.

Neither the Forum nor the Concord is removed from guild loyalties. Councilors carry the interests and histories of the communities that trained them, and influence tends to follow whichever guilds produce the most sought-after and admired works. Debate can be fierce and prolonged, sometimes continuing for weeks over questions that other peoples might settle quickly. The Amdir are accustomed to thinking in generations, but this patience does little to soften disputes over prestige and power. No guild's predominance is permanent, even when its members would prefer otherwise.

Before the Veilsteps

Before the awakening of the Veilsteps, the Guild of Ways was among the wealthiest and most influential institutions of Amdir society. Roads and bridges sustained commerce, migration, and military movement, while mountain crossings opened territories that might otherwise remain isolated. Rulers sought Amdir expertise, and the guild's political standing reflected the necessity of its creations.

The Beacon Dynasty transformed that order. As Veilsteps connected distant places, great roads and bridges lost much of their former strategic importance. They never became obsolete: communities still required local routes, crossings, and access to places beyond the network. The Guild of Ways also adapted to building streets, approaches, and traffic infrastructure around the Steps, where travelers and goods emerged in large numbers. Yet these commissions brought neither the wealth nor the influence once attached to connecting entire regions.

The decline was particularly unsettling because it unfolded within a single Amdir lifetime. The Beacon Dynasty lasted approximately two hundred and seventy years, and a few exceptionally old Amdir may still remember the world before the Steps awakened. For them, the Guild of Ways' lost standing is not an ancient grievance inherited from distant ancestors. It is a change they witnessed, while younger craftsmen have known little else.

The Age of Pillars

The Veilsteps could connect the territories of the Aurelate, but they could not give those territories the buildings through which imperial authority was exercised and displayed. As the Beacon Dynasty expanded, it commissioned great halls, administrative complexes, ceremonial spaces, and monuments that announced its presence. The Guild of Pillars became the favored builder of these works, accumulating wealth, prestige, and close relationships with those who governed.

Towns also grew around important Veilsteps, serving the constant passage of people and goods. The Guild of Ways laid out approaches and managed physical connections, while the Guild of Pillars constructed the civic centers and imposing public buildings that gave these settlements their character. One guild increasingly handled the movement of the empire; the other gave its power a visible and lasting shape.

Over the centuries, the Guild of Pillars became a dominant voice in Amdir politics, its influence reaching into the Concord itself. The fall of the Aurelate did not erase its accomplishments. Though the Ascendancy retained only the former empire's heartland, many monumental works remain there, while others survive in territories beyond its control. The Guild of Pillars sees in these structures the evidence of centuries of achievement and does not consider its authority a temporary privilege to be surrendered when circumstances change.

The Return of the Ways

With the collapse of the Veilsteps, the balance has begun to shift again. Communities that once depended upon the network must now rely upon physical routes, and the diminished Guild of Ways suddenly possesses expertise of urgent importance. Its craftsmen are sought for projects that may reconnect regions and restore the movement of goods and people. To many within the guild, this is an opportunity to recover the position lost during the Beacon Dynasty.

The Guild of Pillars has little intention of yielding. Its members argue that they spent nearly three centuries answering the needs of their age, and that their standing was earned through works that still dominate cities across Ixoria. The Guild of Ways answers that its importance was diminished by the Veilsteps rather than by any failure of craftsmanship, and that the world now needs the connections only its builders can provide.

This rivalry is already felt in the Forum and Concord, where influence has always followed the works that command the greatest regard. It also reaches into apprenticeships and workshops. Some elders remember the Guild of Ways at its height; others spent their lives raising the monuments of the Aurelate. Their apprentices inherit these loyalties along with their techniques, making the conflict both political and personal. The Amdir accept that the needs of the world can change, but accepting change is easier than surrendering the authority one has spent a lifetime acquiring.

An Unsettled Inheritance

At the beginning of the campaign, the Amdir stand between two eras. The monuments of the Aurelate remain, and the Guild of Pillars still holds much of the influence accumulated under the Beacon Dynasty. Yet the roads and bridges that once gave the Guild of Ways its prominence have become essential again, unsettling an order that seemed secure for centuries.

Through these disputes, the older traditions endure. Apprentices continue to follow mentors through work and daily life, craftsmen measure themselves against the demands of their creations, and elders preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost. The question facing the Amdir is not whether they will continue to build, but which works will shape the next age, and which guilds will hold the authority that follows.
