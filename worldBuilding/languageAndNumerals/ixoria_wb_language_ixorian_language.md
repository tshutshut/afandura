# The Ixorian Language

Across Ixoria, a traveler who knows a single tongue can still make themselves understood almost anywhere. Every Kindred keeps a language of its own, and many communities keep several, yet beside them stands a common tongue spoken in markets, courts, harbors and roadside inns from one end of the continent to the other. It is known simply as Ixorian.

Like the numerals that accompany it, Ixorian owes its reach to the Beacon Dynasty, and above all to Caelum the Unifier. As one land after another entered the Aurelate, Caelum required that its laws, its records and its dealings with the empire be conducted in a single language and written in a single script, so that a decree issued in the heartland could be read and understood in the most distant province. The older tongues were never forbidden, and most survive to this day among the peoples who cherish them. Yet it is in Ixorian that strangers speak to one another, and in Ixorian that much of Ixoria is still written, long after the empire that spread it has fallen.

## A Note for Players

For those who play in Ixoria, the language has a simpler origin: Ixorian is English, rebuilt to be consistent. It keeps a reduced set of the sounds of English, and each sound is always pronounced the same way, wherever it appears in a word. Spoken aloud, Ixorian sounds very much like English, though with a distinctive accent of its own. In the tables below, the symbols between slashes are those of the International Phonetic Alphabet, so that every sound can be pronounced without ambiguity.

### Consonants

Ixorian uses twenty-one consonant sounds.

| Sound | Pronunciation | As in |
|---|---|---|
| B | /b/ | base |
| D | /d/ | data |
| F | /f/ | fire |
| G | /g/ | game |
| H | /h/ | hat |
| J | /dʒ/ | jam |
| K | /k/ | case |
| L | /l/ | long |
| M | /m/ | moon |
| N | /n/ | news |
| P | /p/ | potato |
| R | /r/ | rock |
| S | /s/ | seed |
| T | /t/ | tea |
| V | /v/ | vessel |
| W | /w/ | water |
| Y | /j/ | yeast |
| Z | /z/ | zebra |
| SH | /ʃ/ | ship |
| TH | /ð/ | that |
| TH | /θ/ | thought |

Each consonant keeps its sound in every position. G is always hard, as in game and never as in gem, H is always pronounced, and S always hisses, as in seed and never as in rose. Some sounds of English are absent: the ZH of measure has no place in Ixorian, and the NG of sing is pronounced as a full N followed by a G. The CH of check is not a sound of its own either, for it is written and pronounced as a T followed by an SH.

### Vowels

Ixorian uses six vowel sounds.

| Vowel | Pronunciation | As in |
|---|---|---|
| A | /a/ | bat, laugh, far |
| E | /ɛ/ | fair, chair, medical |
| Long I | /iː/ | hear, beat, sleep |
| Short O | /ɔ/ | hot, bro |
| Long O | /ɔː/ | door |
| U | /y/ | as in the French tu |

Several English vowels merge into these six. The short I of sit and lip is pronounced as the E of fair, so that sit sounds much like set. The long U of moon is pronounced as the long O of door, and the vowel of cut, shut and burn becomes U, pronounced as in the French tu. Gliding vowels, such as those of my, day and boy, are written as a vowel followed by a Y. Ixorian has no faint, unstressed vowel like the first sound of about or the last of sofa: every vowel is pronounced in full.

## The Script

Ixorian is written in a flowing italic hand, drawn with a thin pen. Every consonant is a short chain of spikes of three kinds: a tall spike rising two lines above the baseline, a short spike rising one line, and a falling spike plunging one line below it. Each spike rises on a long diagonal to a sharp point, falls straight back along the slant, and curves at its foot into the next. Between every two consonants stands the link, a spike only half a line high. The link belongs to no letter, and it serves only to show where one consonant ends and the next begins.

{{script:strokes}}

The vowels are not written as letters. Ixorian borrows its approach from Arabic, in which short vowels are small marks written above or below the letters rather than letters of their own. Each Ixorian vowel is likewise a mark above or below the consonant it follows. A slanted stroke above stands for A and the same stroke below for E, while a small comma above stands for the short O and a small ring above for U. As in Arabic, a doubled mark gives a longer vowel: two strokes below make the long I, and two commas above make the long O. A word that begins with a vowel opens with the silent carrier, a small loop that holds the vowel's mark, much as the alif does in Arabic.

## The Twenty-One Consonants
