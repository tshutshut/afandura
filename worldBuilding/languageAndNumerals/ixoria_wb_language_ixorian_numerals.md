# Ixorian Numerals

Throughout Ixoria, numbers are written in a system that counts not in tens but in sixty-fours. Its digits are carved on milestones and boundary stones, stamped on coins, painted on the doors of warehouses and written in the ledgers of merchants, magistrates and tax collectors from one end of the continent to the other. Most of those who use them never think to ask where they came from, and those who do ask rarely find an answer.

The origins of the numerals are uncertain, but the reason for their reach is not. They were spread and enforced by the Beacon Dynasty, above all during the reign of Caelum the Unifier. As Caelum brought one land after another into the Aurelate, often through diplomacy rather than conquest, he required that its records, taxes, measures and trade be kept in a single system, so that a sum written in one province could be read without error in any other. Whatever ways of counting the newly joined peoples had used before gave way, slowly but surely, to the numerals of the empire. The Aurelate has since fallen, yet its numerals have outlived it, and they remain in use even in lands that no longer acknowledge any Beacon.

## Reading the Digits

Each Ixorian digit is a single upright stroke, slightly flared at both ends, with six places where a crossbar may be drawn: three on the left side of the stroke and three on the right, at its top, its middle and its foot. Each crossbar ends in a small wedge serif, and each place has its own value. Counted down the left side and then down the right, the values are 1, 2 and 4 on the left, and 8, 16 and 32 on the right. A digit is worth the sum of its crossbars.

The bare stroke, without any crossbar, stands for zero. A single crossbar at the top left stands for one, a single crossbar at the top right for eight, and the two together for nine. With all six crossbars drawn, the digit is worth sixty-three, the largest value a single digit can hold, and it resembles nothing so much as a capital letter crossed through its middle. Sixty-four digits can be written in this way, from {{64:0}} to {{64:63}}.

## Writing Larger Numbers

Larger numbers are written in several digits, read from left to right, each one kept separate from the next. As in other ways of counting, each place is worth more than the place that follows it, though here each place is worth sixty-four times the next rather than ten. Sixty-four itself is therefore written as a one followed by a bare stroke, {{64:64}}, just as ten is written as a one followed by a zero. One hundred is written {{64:100}}, a one and a thirty-six, for one sixty-four and thirty-six more.
