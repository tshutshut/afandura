First Harmony Undone

The Red Pilgrims teach that there was a time when the material world and the ethereal world were not divided. The boundary between them was known as the Veil, though in those elder days it was not a barrier in the manner it is understood today. It was a living threshold through which the two worlds touched and, at times, passed into one another.

Things of the ethereal world could enter the material without tearing it apart, just as things of the material world could pass into the ethereal and leave something of themselves behind. The presence of one within the other was not regarded as unnatural. The two worlds existed in harmony, distinct yet joined, each giving and receiving from the other according to the ancient order. The Pilgrims call this time the First Harmony.

They do not remember it as an age without suffering or danger. The ethereal world was not without its perils, nor was the material world without its sorrows. Yet neither sought to consume the other, and neither sought to break the bond between them. The Veil endured because the worlds respected the boundary that joined them.

Then came the Hunger Beyond. The oldest traditions do not agree upon what the Hunger Beyond was before it reached toward the material world. Some describe it as a creature of the deepest ethereal reaches, while others speak of something older than either world, a thing for which there was no rightful place in either. The Red Pilgrims preserve these differences without attempting to settle them.

They agree only upon what the Hunger Beyond desired. It desired the material world, not to dwell within it, nor to know it, but to consume it. The Hunger Beyond reached toward the Veil and began to tear. It bore two hands, each with five fingers, and three mouths. With its ten fingers and three mouths, it seized the Veil at thirteen points and tore and bit its way toward the material world. At each point the Veil was broken, and at each point the worlds were wounded.

Yet the Hunger Beyond did not understand the Law of Returning. The material and ethereal worlds had been joined since the beginning. What was done to one could return upon the other. A wound made in one world was reflected in the other, and the violence of the crossing was carried back upon the one who had caused it.

The Hunger Beyond tore at the world, and the world wounded the Hunger Beyond in return. Thirteen wounds were made before the Hunger Beyond could tear further. The violence had weakened it, and the places through which it had sought to enter had become bound to it. What had been intended as openings became restraints. What had been meant as a passage became a prison.

The wounds hardened around the Hunger Beyond, and it was left trapped within the Veil it had tried to destroy. The Red Pilgrims call this the Binding. They believe that the Hunger Beyond remains there still, held fast by the wounds of its own making.

The thirteen wounds became joined to one another, forming a continuous boundary around the place where the Hunger Beyond was trapped. This boundary became known as the Red Line. The Wounds are its points of binding, while the Line is the seam that joins them.

The Pilgrims believe that the Binding changed the nature of the world itself. Before the coming of the Hunger Beyond, the material and ethereal worlds could mingle freely. After the Binding, that exchange ceased. The ethereal could no longer pass freely into the material, nor could the material pass freely into the ethereal.

The separation that exists today is therefore not regarded by the Red Pilgrims as the natural order of creation. It is the consequence of the Hunger Beyond and the thirteen wounds it made upon the Veil. For this reason, the Wounds are not to be healed. They are to be maintained.

The thirteen sites must be visited in their proper order, and the Red Line must be walked without end. The thirteenth Wound returns to the first, and so no pilgrimage has a final destination. Each completed circuit is the beginning of another.

The purpose of the journey is not arrival, but preservation. The Red Line remains because the Wounds remain. The worlds remain apart because the Line remains. And the Line remains because it is walked.

The Red Book

The oldest teachings of the Red Pilgrims are preserved in the Red Book, a collection of sacred writings whose earliest passages are believed to originate from the first keepers of the Wounds. Its oldest words have been carried through generations by recitation and careful copying, and their meaning is taught alongside the rites of the pilgrimage.

One of its foundational passages is traditionally recited when the nature of the Veil, the Wounds, and the duty of the Pilgrims are taught:

In the elder days, ere the Wound was made,
the worlds were joined beneath the Veil.

The flesh knew the unseen,
and the unseen touched the flesh.

Neither world held dominion over the other,
for such was the First Harmony.

Then came the Hunger Beyond.

It beheld the world of flesh
and desired to consume it.

Ten fingers did it raise against the Veil,
and three mouths did it open.

Thirteen places did it seize.

Thirteen wounds did it make.

Yet the Hunger Beyond knew not the Law of Returning.

For what is done unto one world
is carried unto the other.

The hand that tears shall be torn.
The mouth that bites shall bear the wound.

Thus was the Hunger Beyond wounded
by the wound of its own making.

And when the thirteenth wound was made,
the tearing ceased.

The wounds held fast to the Hunger Beyond,
and that which it had opened became its prison.

It could neither enter
nor depart.

The Wounds remained open,
yet the passage was closed.

The worlds were parted,
and the Red Line was made.

Therefore let none heal the Wounds.

Let none abandon the Line.

Let none forget what lies beyond the Veil.

For the Wounds are the bindings.

The Line is the chain.

Walk the Wounds in their order.

Walk them without ending.

For while the Line is kept,
the worlds remain apart.
