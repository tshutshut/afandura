# The Ballad of Maric

*A song of Devil's Luck*

Maric went to sleep a man,
Beneath his humble roof,
And dreamed of naught more strange or grand
Than life’s uncertain proof.

But morning found him altered quite,
With horns upon his brow,
A tail curled underneath the sheet—
And stranger still somehow.

His mother would not meet his gaze,
His father barred the door.
The neighbors whispered through the lane,
“Maric is man no more!”

He took his coat, he took his purse,
And took the road alone.
He knew not where he’d rest that night,
Nor where he’d call his own.

And that was Maric's luck!

He stopped beside a crowded street,
And checked his purse with care,
But when he looked, the purse was gone—
No thief was lurking there.

The thief was quick, the thief was lean,
A shadow swift of hand;
He slipped unseen through market throngs
And vanished from the land.

But while the thief was counting coins
And laughing at his prize,
Another thief came strolling by,
With mischief in his eyes.

He snatched the purse and hurried on,
And vanished down the way;
It seemed that luck had favored him
Since first he saw the day.

Maric came upon a narrow lane,
And there he spied the thief,
Who lounged beside the market wall,
Counting his coins with glee.

He knew his purse by leather worn,
By clasp and battered thread;
He reached to seize it from the thief,
Who sprang up and fled.

They grappled in the crowded street,
And Maric cried, “That’s mine!”
A guard came running at the din,
And seized them both in time.

“That purse is mine!” the thief cried out,
“He seeks to steal my gold!”
The guard looked first upon Maric,
Then watched the thief unfold.

For Maric bore the horns and tail,
And folk were quick to judge;
The thief was freed, while Maric found
Himself before the judge.

He swore the purse was truly his,
He swore the tale was false;
But none would heed the horned man’s plea,
Nor pause to hear his cause.

And that was Maric's luck.

They chained poor Maric to a cart
And sent him off to jail;
The driver cracked his leather whip,
The horses snorted pale.

For by the roadside, green and plump,
There grew a cabbage head;
The horses saw only supper there,
And cared not where they’d led.

The driver pulled the reins in vain,
The beasts refused to steer;
They kicked the cart, then kicked again,
And chaos followed near.

The wagon struck a market stall,
The stall struck someone's door;
The door brought down a stack of crates,
And crates went to the floor.

A horse ran left, A horse ran right,
The driver lost his seat;
The cart went bounding down the street,
And nothing could compete.

A window shattered by the cart,
And Maric sailed right through;
He crashed into an inn below,
And landed in its stew.

The guards presumed him dead and gone,
And hurried on their way;
The innkeeper just stared at Maric,
Who shouted, “What a day!”

And that was Maric's luck.

The innkeeper had lost his cook,
Who'd left the week before;
His kitchen needed working hands,
His cellar needed more.

“You've broken through my kitchen,
You've ruined half my stew;
Since you have landed in my pot,
I suppose I'll hire you.”

Maric had never cooked before,
He told him so quite plain;
The innkeeper replied, “Then learn,”
And handed him some grain.

For one short day poor Maric worked,
And did the best he might;
By noon the kitchen smoked apace,
By dusk the roof alight.

The curtains caught, the rafters blazed,
The chimney filled with flame;
Maric fled out into the street,
Never looked behind again.

He fled into the street alone,
With smoke still at his back,
And straight into a wedding train
That blocked the whole main track.

The fiddler dropped his fiddle there,
The bride's old father stared;
He looked at Maric, black with smoke,
And thought him well prepared.

And that was Maric's luck.

“Come, friend!” the father cried aloud,
“You're late, but just in time!”
They placed a hat upon his head
And filled his cup with wine.

Someone pressed the fiddle forth,
Someone brought him bread;
“Play us something for the bride!”
Poor Maric shook his head.

“I cannot play, I cannot sing,
I know not any tune.”
But wedding folk are generous,
And noise becomes a boon.

He drew the bow across the strings,
The fiddle gave a shriek;
The bride began to laugh aloud,
The groom turned rather bleak.

The uncle danced upon a chair,
The groom fell in the punch.
The bride began to laugh so hard
she nearly lost her lunch.

Maric sought the nearest exit,
But fortune barred the way;
He caught the bride's long wedding dress
And pulled it clean away.

The bride cried out, the guests cried out,
The groom cried out as well;
Maric ran toward the balcony
As fast as he could tell.

He reached the window, lost his step,
And tumbled through the frame;
He fell three stories through the air
But fortune struck again.

A dragon passing overhead
Was flying low that day;
It caught poor Maric by his coat
And carried him away.

And that was Maric's luck.

The dragon flew for miles and miles,
While Maric clung on tight;
He prayed that it would set him down
Before the fall of night.

Then suddenly the dragon sneezed,
A thunderous, mighty sound;
It shook its wings, and Maric flew
Unceremoniously down.

He fell toward a farmer's field
And thought his end was nigh;
But fortune placed a cart of hay
Beneath him by and by.

The farmer stopped and stared at him,
While Maric stared back too.
The farmer asked, “Are you all right?”
“I think I am,” he said. “Are you?”

The farmer scratched his whiskered chin
And looked him up and down.
“You need a place to sleep tonight?”
“I do,” said Maric. “Yes.”

“You know the work of field and farm?”
Maric said simply "No".
The farmer pondered for a while,
Then shrugged and said, “Well, so?”

“My last hand knew no more than you,
And stayed with me a year.”
He pointed toward the barn and said,
“You may as well stay here.”

And that was Maric's luck.

So Maric stayed upon the farm
And learned to mend a fence;
He learned to milk, he learned to plough,
And slowly gained good sense.

He never robbed a royal court,
Nor sought a crown or throne;
He never slew a mighty beast,
Nor claimed a jewel his own.

He simply lived from year to year,
And worked beneath the sun;
And every time the world went wrong,
Somehow he came out one.

Some say he lived to ninety years,
Some say a hundred three;
Some say the dragon came back once
And drank with him some tea.

Some say he grew a wealthy man,
Some say he died quite poor;
Some say he never left the farm,
Some say he walked once more.

Now every Ilie knows the tale,
And every Ilie knows:

The road may twist, the road may turn,
Even betray your stride;
But curse it not for where it leads,
You may yet thrive beside.

A purse may vanish from your hand,
A prison gate may close;
A wedding gown may leave its bride,
A dragon might impose.

So curse not fate, nor curse the road,
Nor curse the stars above;
For what begins in misery
May end in fortune's love.

And should the worst befall you yet,
And every plan go wrong—
Remember Maric walked those roads,
And somehow still walked on.

When fortune turns her face away,
And leaves you in the rain,
Do not be quick to curse your lot—
The road may turn again.
