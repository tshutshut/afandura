# The Ballad of Maric

*A song of Devil's Luck*

Maric went to sleep a man,
beneath his roof and bed,
and dreamed of nothing stranger
than the life that lay ahead.

But morning found him changed somehow,
with horns upon his brow,
a tail curled underneath the sheets,
and stranger still somehow.

His mother would not look at him,
his father barred the door.
The neighbors whispered in the lane
as Maric stood outside once more.

He took his coat, he took his purse,
and took the road alone.
He did not know where he would sleep,
or where he would call home.

And that was the beginning
of Maric's unlucky road.
He walked until the morning passed,
and hunger claimed its toll.

He stopped beside a crowded street,
and checked his purse with care.
But when he looked, the purse was gone,
and no thief could be seen there.

The thief was quick, the thief was thin,
the thief was quick of hand.
He vanished through the market crowd
and disappeared from land.

But while the thief was counting coins
and laughing at his prize,
another thief came down the road
with mischief in his eyes.

He took the first thief's stolen purse
and hurried on his way.
But luck, it seems, had followed him
since early in the day.

A city guard came round the bend
and caught the second thief.
He searched the purse, he found Maric's name,
and thought the matter brief.

“You'll come with me,” the guard declared.
Maric answered, “But I'm robbed!”

The guard replied, “Tell that to the judge.”

And that was Maric's luck.

They chained poor Maric to a cart
and sent him off to jail.
The driver cracked his leather whip,
the horse began to wail.

For by the roadside, green and proud,
there stood a cabbage head.
The horse saw only dinner there;
the horse ignored the dead.

The driver pulled the reins in vain,
the horse refused to steer.
It kicked the cart, it kicked again,
and chaos followed near.

The wagon struck a market stall,
the stall struck someone's door.
The door struck down a stack of crates,
and crates went to the floor.

The horse ran left, the wagon right,
the driver lost his seat.
The prison cart struck one more wall
and bounced into the street.

The floor beneath poor Maric broke.
He vanished from their sight.
He landed in an innkeeper's stew,
which saved his life outright.

The guards believed him dead, of course.
The innkeeper did not.

He stared at Maric, dripping stew,
and shouted, “What you've got!”

And that was Maric's luck.

The innkeeper had lost his cook,
who'd left the week before.
His kitchen needed working hands,
his cellar needed more.

“You've broken through my kitchen floor,
you've ruined half my stew.
Since you are standing in my pot,
I suppose I'll hire you.”

Maric had never cooked before.
He said as much quite plain.

The innkeeper replied, “Then learn.”

And handed him a grain.

For three short days Maric worked
and tried to keep things right.
Then smoke began to fill the hall,
and someone shouted, “FIRE!”

The kitchen burned, the curtains burned,
the roof began to glow.
Maric ran out through the front door
with nowhere else to go.

He fled into the street alone,
with smoke behind his back,
and straight into a wedding band
that blocked the whole main track.

The fiddler dropped his fiddle.
The bride's old father raised his cup.
He looked at Maric, dressed in smoke—

And that was Maric's luck.

“Come, friend!” the father shouted.
“You're late, but just in time!”
They put a hat upon his head
and handed him some wine.

Someone gave him a fiddle.
Someone gave him bread.
Someone asked him for a wedding song.
Maric shook his head.

He could not play the fiddle.
He could barely hold the tune.
But weddings have a strange belief
that noise is music soon.

So everyone began to dance,
and Maric danced along.
He struck the fiddle once or twice,
which made the whole thing worse.

The bride's old uncle joined the dance.
The groom fell in the punch.
The bride began to laugh so hard
she nearly lost her lunch.

The father called for one more round.
The musicians played faster.
Maric tried to leave the room,
but stepped upon a plaster.

The floor gave way beneath his feet.
The wedding gave a scream.
And Maric disappeared below
the tables and the stream.

He landed in the dark below,
where rats had made their home.
But rats were not what waited there—

A smuggler's tunnel wound beneath the stone.

And that was Maric's luck.

The tunnel ran beneath the town,
beneath the stones and street.
It twisted past forgotten wells
and cellars dark and deep.

Maric followed where it went,
for going back seemed worse.
He hoped to find a quiet road
and maybe find a purse.

At last he saw the daylight shine.
He climbed toward open air.

He emerged beside a prison gate.

The guards were waiting there.

They saw his horns. They saw his face.
They saw the road below.
One guard shouted, “There he is!”

Maric whispered, “Oh no.”

They seized him once again,
and dragged him to the square.
The captain said, “You escaped before!”

Maric said, “I wasn't there.”

The captain did not understand.
The guards did not care.
They put him beside a prison cart
and tied him to a chair.

And that was Maric's luck.

Beside the cart there stood a horse,
a nervous thing and gray.
It stamped its hoof, it shook its head,
it did not like the day.

The captain shouted at the men.
The guards began to shout.
The horse decided it had heard
quite enough and started out.

It kicked the cart. The cart rolled free.
It struck the prison gate.
The gate shook once, the hinges screamed,
and then it met its fate.

The gate fell inward with a crash.
The guards all scattered wide.
Maric was standing just beyond it,
with nobody at his side.

He looked at all the broken wood.
He looked at all the guards.
He dusted off his coat and said,

“I think I'll leave the yard.”

And so he walked away from jail,
though this much must be said:

Maric escaped the prison
without ever going in.

And that was Maric's luck.

He left that city by the road
and walked for many days,
until he reached a greater town
of towers, walls, and gates.

A royal procession filled the street
with banners, drums, and gold.
Maric saw it coming near
and thought, “I'll not be told.”

He turned into an alleyway
to let the nobles pass.
The alley ended at a door.
The door was made of brass.

The door led through a passage,
the passage led to kitchens.
A cook looked at Maric's horns
and shouted, “Delivery!”

He placed a basket in his arms.
It was full of oranges.
Maric tried to tell him,
“But I have brought you none!”

The cook replied, “Then take these upstairs.”

And off the poor man ran.

Maric carried oranges through
the corridors of the court,
until a finely dressed lord saw him
and shouted, “STOP THAT SORT!”

The guards came running.
The noble pointed at Maric.

“Assassin!”

Maric dropped an orange.

It rolled beneath a chair.

And that was Maric's luck.

The guards came from every side.
Maric ran toward the wall.
A soldier slipped upon an orange,
and a second soldier fell.

A third one crashed into a brazier.
The brazier struck a curtain.
The curtain caught the tapestry.
The tapestry caught the hall.

The hall caught half the palace.
The palace caught fire.
Maric ran beneath the smoke,
with guards behind his feet.

He found a window, climbed outside,
and thought himself complete.
The palace burned behind him.
The bells began to ring.

Maric looked down from the window.

Something had spread its wing.

He had no time to wonder
what had brought it there that night.

He jumped.

And landed on a dragon.

And that was Maric's luck.

The dragon did not appreciate
a passenger on its back.
It roared and climbed into the sky.
Maric held on to its neck.

They flew above the palace walls,
above the city roofs,
above the river and the fields,
above the startled flocks.

Maric had no saddle.
Maric had no reins.
Maric had only both his hands
and several thousand fears.

The dragon flew for miles and miles.
Maric prayed it would descend.
The dragon sneezed.

Maric flew.

He fell toward a farmer's field
and thought his luck had ended.
But beneath him lay a cart of hay,
and Maric landed softly there.

The farmer stopped and stared at him.
Maric stared back too.
The farmer asked, “Are you all right?”

Maric said, “I think I am.”

The farmer looked at the smoking city
far behind the hill.
He looked at Maric in his hay.
The dragon was gone still.

The farmer scratched his beard and said,
“You need a place to stay?”

Maric said, “Yes.”

“You know how to farm?”

“No.”

The farmer thought about it.

“Neither did my last man.”

And that was Maric's luck.

So Maric stayed upon the farm
and learned to mend a fence.
He learned to milk, he learned to plow,
and learned the farmer's sense.

He never robbed a royal court.
He never sought a throne.
He never slew a mighty beast.
He never carved his stone.

He simply lived from year to year,
and worked beneath the sun.
And every time the world went wrong,
somehow he came out the one.

Some say he lived to ninety years.
Some say a hundred three.
Some say the dragon came back once
and asked him for its fee.

Some say he became a wealthy man.
Some say he died poor.
Some say he never left that farm.
Some say he walked once more.

But every Ilie knows the tale,
and every Ilie knows:

When every road is blocked ahead,
there's always one that goes.

So if your purse is stolen,
if your prison wagon breaks,
if your wedding floor gives way,
if a dragon makes mistakes,

If palace fires chase after you,
if fortune's running amok—

Don't curse the road.

Don't curse your fate.

**And that was Maric's luck.**
