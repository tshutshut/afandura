Ixoria — Classes

Classes in Ixoria are story-driven archetypes rather than rigid professions. Choosing a class should say something meaningful about how a character has lived, what they have learned, and how they tend to approach the world. The questions beneath each class are intended to help players establish that history.

Barbarian

A Barbarian is someone whose approach to danger and adversity relies upon physical power, endurance, instinct, and an ability to continue when others would falter. The name does not imply stupidity, ignorance, or an inability to reason. A Barbarian may be intelligent, perceptive, and even highly knowledgeable, but their life and experiences have taught them to confront problems directly rather than delicately.

Barbarians tend to lack social refinement. They are often blunt, direct, impatient with elaborate etiquette, and sometimes vulgar. They may understand what others expect of them perfectly well while seeing little reason to disguise their thoughts or soften their words. This can make them uncomfortable participants in situations requiring diplomacy, subtle persuasion, or careful social maneuvering.

Physical confrontation, however, is familiar territory. Barbarians tend to relish opportunities to test their strength, endurance, and courage, and may feel more comfortable facing an immediate physical threat than navigating a room full of carefully chosen words. A player choosing Barbarian should therefore explain what kind of life produced this combination of resilience, directness, and comfort with physical conflict.

Questions to Consider

  • What conditions or experiences shaped your character into a Barbarian?
  • Where did their physical resilience come from? Was it trained, or simply necessary for survival?
  • What kinds of conflicts have they experienced?
  • Is there a particular battle, fight, or confrontation they remember vividly? Do they remember it with pride, fondness, regret, or fear?
  • How does your character fight? Have they developed a recognizable fighting style or method?
  • What do they enjoy about physical confrontation: the danger, the challenge, the competition, the release, or something else?
  • How does their bluntness affect their relationships with other people?
  • How do they react when faced with a problem that cannot be solved through strength or direct confrontation?

Bard

A Bard is, above all, an entertainer and performer. They have learned to use artistic expression not merely to display skill, but to awaken emotion in others. Through their performance, a Bard can change the atmosphere of a room, capture an audience's attention, strengthen an existing feeling, or draw emotions to the surface that those listening or watching might otherwise have kept buried.

The form this performance takes is entirely up to the character. A Bard might sing, chant, play an instrument, dance, tell stories, recite poetry, act, paint, or practice another form of artistic expression. Some may combine several disciplines. What matters is not the medium, but the character's ability to communicate through it and affect those who experience their work.

Nor must a Bard's influence be cheerful or reassuring. A performance might inspire courage, hope, enthusiasm, or joy, but it might just as easily evoke sorrow, melancholy, nostalgia, anger, unease, or fear. A skilled Bard understands that art can uplift as readily as it can unsettle.

Choosing Bard therefore requires more than simply declaring that the character is charismatic or knows how to play an instrument. Their art should form a meaningful part of who they are. The player should consider what their character performs, how they developed their craft, and what makes their performances capable of reaching others.

Questions to Consider

  • What is your Bard's primary form of artistic expression?
  • How did they learn their craft, and how long have they practiced it?
  • Why is performing so important to them? What does their art mean to them personally?
  • Does their performance require anything specific, such as an instrument, costumes, paints, props, or other equipment?
  • Do they have a particular style, appearance, or persona when performing?
  • What does their repertoire consist of?
  • Where does their material come from? Do they perform works belonging to their own culture, borrow from other Kindreds and traditions, or create their own?
  • What emotions do they most enjoy provoking in an audience?
  • What kind of performer are they: humorous and exuberant, solemn and dramatic, provocative, melancholic, intimate, frightening, or something else entirely?
  • How comfortable are they when they are not the center of attention?
  • How do they use their social confidence when confronted with hostility or disagreement?
  • When a situation becomes physical, do they avoid confrontation, attempt to talk their way out of it, rely upon others, or reluctantly fight?
  • Is there a particular performance they are especially proud of, or one that went terribly wrong?
  • What audience does your Bard most enjoy performing for, and is there an audience they struggle to reach?

Devout

A Devout is someone whose life is deeply shaped by religious devotion. They have a principal source of faith—a deity, sacred figure, or established religious tradition—and have dedicated a significant part of their life to understanding, practicing, and serving that faith. Simply believing in a deity or participating occasionally in religious customs does not make someone Devout. Their devotion must be substantial enough to influence how they live.

A Devout follows the traditions, laws, rituals, duties, and restrictions associated with their faith. These obligations may influence what they eat, how they dress, how they treat others, the rituals they perform, the places they visit, or the actions they consider acceptable. Their religion therefore exists not merely as part of their background, but as something that regularly affects their decisions.

The Devout also seek to reinforce their faith through experience. They may interpret events through the teachings of their religion, search for meaning in hardship or coincidence, and invoke their deity during moments of fear, uncertainty, gratitude, inspiration, or need. Prayer and religious observance may form an ordinary part of their daily lives rather than something reserved for temples or ceremonies.

The intensity of this devotion can vary considerably. One Devout might be compassionate and contemplative, another austere and disciplined, while another might be zealous or uncompromising. Strong devotion should be grounded in the character's history and beliefs. The stronger the character's conviction, the more clearly the player should understand what they believe, why they believe it, and what their faith demands of them.

Faith and magic are entirely separate in Ixoria. Religious devotion does not grant magical ability, and possessing magic does not imply any religious connection.

Questions to Consider

  • What deity, sacred figure, or religious tradition does your character follow?
  • Why did they devote themselves to this particular faith?
  • What does their faith require of them in everyday life?
  • What rituals or observances do they practice regularly?
  • What duties, prohibitions, or restrictions do they follow?
  • Have they sacrificed anything important in order to remain faithful?
  • How do they treat people who do not share their beliefs?
  • In what situations do they find themselves praying or invoking their faith most often?
  • What experiences have strengthened their faith?
  • Has their devotion ever brought them into conflict with another person, institution, or community?
  • Are they gentle in their beliefs, strict, zealous, contemplative, or something else?
  • What could cause them to question their faith?

Druid

A Druid is someone whose bond with the living world is central to who they are. That connection is not merely academic or a matter of belief. It is something they seek out and maintain through direct experience. Druids prefer to encounter nature rather than study it from a distance: observing animals, tending plants, experiencing weather, travelling through open spaces, and learning the patterns of the living world firsthand.

A Druid's connection may center upon a particular aspect of nature. One may feel most at home among forests and trees, another beside the sea, beneath an open sky, among mountains, across plains, or in the company of particular animals. Their knowledge and habits should reflect the part of the natural world with which they have actually developed a relationship.

Druids often develop personal rituals, routines, or practices that nurture this connection. These need not be religious. They might involve tending a place, observing a natural cycle, caring for animals, undertaking seasonal journeys, gathering particular plants, watching the weather, or simply spending periods of time alone outdoors.

A Druid is therefore not defined by possessing abstract knowledge about nature, but by wanting to experience and participate in it. Being confined for too long behind a desk, within a study, or away from the living world should feel fundamentally contrary to the life they have chosen.

Questions to Consider

  • What part of the natural world does your Druid feel most connected to, and why?
  • Is their connection centered on forests, mountains, oceans, rivers, open fields, the sky, weather, animals, plants, or something else?
  • Are there particular animals or forms of life toward which they feel especially drawn?
  • Tell of a moment when your character felt particularly connected to the natural world.
  • Where does your Druid feel most at home?
  • What does your character do regularly to nurture their connection with nature?
  • Do they have personal rituals, routines, or seasonal practices?
  • How did those practices develop?
  • Did someone teach them about the natural world, or did they learn primarily through experience?
  • Is there a place in nature that is particularly important to them?
  • How do they react when they are forced to remain away from nature for an extended period?
  • Is there any aspect of the natural world that frightens, unsettles, or challenges them?

Fighter

A Fighter is someone who has deliberately chosen to pursue martial skill as a discipline. Unlike a Barbarian, whose prowess may emerge from hardship, instinct, or the conditions in which they lived, a Fighter has consciously sought training. Their abilities are the product of study, instruction, repetition, and practical experience under one or more masters, schools, military traditions, or other martial doctrines.

This training should have given the Fighter a recognizable specialty. They may have devoted themselves to a particular weapon, combination of weapons, fighting style, or approach to combat. A Fighter may broaden their abilities over time, but their character should reflect what they were actually taught and what they have practiced enough to consider themselves proficient in.

A Fighter's training also extends beyond physical technique. The tradition in which they learned should have imparted a doctrine: principles concerning how their skills are practiced, understood, and applied. These principles need not be moral or honorable. They might concern discipline, efficiency, loyalty, hierarchy, patience, aggression, restraint, or any other ideas embedded within their training. What matters is that the Fighter has absorbed something from their martial education that continues to influence their behavior and decisions.

Choosing Fighter therefore means giving the character a genuine martial history. Their training should have teachers, methods, experiences, and consequences. Their abilities were earned deliberately, and the player should be able to explain the path through which they acquired them.

Questions to Consider

  • Why did your character deliberately choose to pursue martial training?
  • Who trained them? Did they have a single master, several masters, or belong to a particular school or tradition?
  • Did they remain within one martial tradition, or move between different teachers and schools?
  • What fighting style did they learn?
  • What weapon, weapons, or other form of combat do they specialize in?
  • What does their martial doctrine teach beyond the physical techniques of fighting?
  • What principles from that doctrine does your Fighter still uphold in their daily life?
  • Did their training impose any duties, restrictions, loyalties, debts, or expectations upon them?
  • Have they ever practiced their skills professionally—as a soldier, guard, mercenary, duelist, protector, or in some other capacity?
  • Who have they served, fought for, or been hired by?
  • What experience best demonstrated the value of their training?
  • Is there a battle, duel, assignment, or confrontation of which they are particularly proud?
  • Have they ever used their martial skill in a way they later regretted? Who suffered because of it?
  • How has actual violence changed their understanding of the doctrine they were taught?

Vindicator

A Vindicator is a warrior whose martial life is inseparable from their faith. They have devoted considerable time to learning how to fight, developing proficiency with particular weapons, techniques, or martial disciplines. Unlike a Fighter, however, martial mastery is not an end in itself. The Vindicator fights because they believe their faith demands action.

A Vindicator possesses a strong understanding of right and wrong shaped by their religious convictions. They believe certain people, principles, places, traditions, or ways of life deserve protection, while other actions or forces must be confronted. These convictions are not merely philosophical. A Vindicator believes there are circumstances in which their ideals must be upheld by force.

For this reason, Vindicators often take to the road. They travel through Ixoria pursuing causes, answering perceived injustices, defending what they consider sacred, confronting enemies of their beliefs, and seeking opportunities to further their religious mission. Their devotion is active and militant: where a Devout may practice their faith through observance, ritual, and personal conduct, a Vindicator carries that faith into conflict.

This certainty does not mean that the Vindicator is objectively correct. Their understanding of righteousness belongs to the character and may be questioned by companions, strangers, or even other followers of the same faith. A Vindicator may be admired as a protector by some and regarded as fanatical, dangerous, or misguided by others. What defines the class is the Vindicator's own conviction that their cause is righteous and that their martial abilities are necessary to serve it.

Questions to Consider

  • What faith does your Vindicator follow?
  • What teachings or principles of that faith most strongly shape their understanding of right and wrong?
  • What do they believe must be protected?
  • What do they believe must be opposed?
  • Why do they believe physical force is sometimes necessary to uphold their faith?
  • Who taught them to fight, and under what circumstances did their martial training begin?
  • What weapons, fighting styles, or martial techniques have they mastered?
  • Is their fighting tradition itself connected to their faith, or did they acquire those skills separately?
  • Does their faith impose rules concerning when, why, or against whom they may use violence?
  • What mission or cause currently drives them to travel through Ixoria?
  • Describe an important past mission in which they fought to uphold their beliefs. Who or what did they confront, and why?
  • What happened during that mission, and what consequences followed?
  • What past conflict do they consider their greatest triumph in service of their faith?
  • Have they ever undertaken a mission that ended badly or produced consequences they did not expect?
  • Have they ever been forced to question whether an action they considered righteous truly served their faith?
  • How do they respond to people who share their religion but disagree with their militant interpretation of it?
  • What, if anything, could cause them to abandon a mission they sincerely believe to be righteous?

Adventurer

An Adventurer is someone who has chosen a life of travel, exploration, and personal freedom. They are drawn toward unfamiliar places and new experiences, preferring the uncertainty of the road to the predictability of a settled existence. For an Adventurer, travelling is not simply a means of reaching a destination. The journey itself is an important part of who they are.

Through experience, Adventurers develop practical knowledge of the world around them. They learn to navigate difficult terrain, observe changes in weather, understand the behavior of animals, find food and shelter, recognize dangers, and adapt when plans inevitably go wrong. Their knowledge of nature may sometimes resemble that of a Druid, but the motivation is different. A Druid seeks a connection with the natural world; an Adventurer learns about it by travelling through it, surviving within it, and experiencing everything it has to offer.

Most Adventurers develop particular affinities based upon where their travels have taken them. One might know mountains and cold climates intimately, while another may be most comfortable crossing plains, forests, deserts, coastlines, or warmer regions. Their experience should therefore reflect where they have actually been rather than granting them universal knowledge of every environment.

An Adventurer should also have stories. Previous journeys, expeditions, discoveries, accidents, encounters, and unexpected events have shaped them. These experiences need not involve combat. What matters is that the character has genuinely ventured into the world, experienced something worth remembering, and returned carrying stories that have become part of their identity.

Above all, Adventurers value their freedom. They may travel with companions, accept work, pursue discoveries, or remain somewhere for a time, but eventually the desire to move returns. There is always another road, another landscape, another settlement, or another story waiting somewhere beyond what they already know.

Questions to Consider

  • Why did your character originally choose a wandering life?
  • Where in Ixoria have they travelled extensively?
  • Which regions or routes do they know particularly well?
  • What kinds of terrain or climate are they most comfortable travelling through?
  • Is there an environment they dislike or have little experience navigating?
  • What practical survival skills have they developed through their travels?
  • Do they usually travel alone, with companions, with caravans, or as part of organized expeditions?
  • Have they ever worked as a guide, scout, explorer, courier, hunter, or in another profession connected to travel?
  • Describe a memorable expedition or journey from their past. Where did they go, and why?
  • What unexpected event occurred during that journey?
  • How did that experience change them?
  • What is one story from their travels that they particularly enjoy telling other people?
  • Is there a place they discovered that they desperately want to visit again?
  • Is there somewhere in Ixoria they have always wanted to explore but have never reached?
  • What possessions or equipment do they consider indispensable while travelling?
  • What does personal freedom mean to them?
  • Why have they never chosen—or been able—to settle permanently in one place?
  • What would have to happen for them to finally abandon the wandering life?

Rogue

A Rogue is someone who chooses to live according to their own rules. Laws, traditions, authorities, and social expectations do not automatically command their obedience. Instead, a Rogue decides for themselves which rules deserve to be respected, which can be ignored, and which should actively be broken.

This does not necessarily make a Rogue a criminal. One might become a thief, smuggler, swindler, or outlaw, while another could be a vigilante who breaks established laws in pursuit of their own understanding of justice. Others may simply be free spirits who refuse to allow institutions or conventions to dictate how they live. What matters is that the Rogue possesses a strong sense of personal independence and has developed their own boundaries concerning what they will and will not do.

Adaptability and resourcefulness are central to the Rogue's identity. Rather than relying upon established procedures or rigid doctrines, Rogues improvise. They observe their surroundings, exploit opportunities, circumvent obstacles, and find unconventional solutions when circumstances change. Their ability to survive difficult situations often comes from being willing to attempt approaches that others would never consider.

A Rogue must also possess practical skills that support this lifestyle. These specialties will differ considerably between characters. One Rogue might be an accomplished escape artist, while another may understand locks and mechanisms, detect and disable traps, move unnoticed, disguise themselves, deceive others, forge documents, perform sleight of hand, or possess some entirely different specialty.

These abilities should not exist without explanation. The player should establish how their Rogue acquired their skills, how they practiced them, and experiences in which those abilities proved important. A Rogue's specialties should emerge naturally from the life they have lived.

Ultimately, the Rogue is defined not by criminality but by independence, adaptability, and the ability to rely upon their own skills when established rules and conventional solutions prove inadequate.

Questions to Consider

  • What kind of lifestyle does your Rogue lead?
  • What rules or principles have they established for themselves?
  • Which laws, traditions, or expectations imposed by others do they refuse to respect?
  • Where does your Rogue draw the line? Is there something they simply will not do?
  • Were they always this independent, or did something happen that changed their relationship with rules and authority?
  • What practical skills does your Rogue specialize in?
  • How did they acquire those skills?
  • Who, if anyone, taught them?
  • How long have they practiced their specialties?
  • Describe an occasion when one of those skills proved particularly useful.
  • What experience best demonstrates their adaptability or resourcefulness?
  • Describe a situation in which they escaped or overcame a problem through an unconventional solution.
  • Have their choices ever placed them in conflict with authorities, institutions, or their community?
  • Have they ever deliberately broken an important rule because they believed it should not apply to them? What happened?
  • Does your Rogue generally operate alone, or have they worked with others?
  • Have they ever belonged to a gang, band, network, organization, group of vigilantes, or some other community?
  • Who were their most important companions, and what became of those relationships?
  • Is there a defining moment from their past that shaped the person they are today?
  • Has their independence ever cost them something or someone important?
  • What, if anything, could persuade them to follow somebody else's rules?

Mage

A Mage is someone born with an affinity for magic who has chosen to understand, study, and deliberately develop that ability. Magical potential exists from birth, but it does not necessarily express itself immediately. A Mage may first experience magic during childhood, adolescence, or considerably later in life. What defines the Mage is not when their magic first manifests, but what they choose to do once they become aware of it.

For a Mage, magic is something to be understood. They approach their abilities deliberately, experimenting with them, observing their effects, testing their limitations, and gradually learning how to reproduce desired results. Practice and reflection are therefore fundamental to their development. Even when a Mage learns entirely through personal experimentation, their progress is the result of conscious effort rather than simply surrendering to instinct.

Every Mage possesses an innate magical affinity. This affinity determines the kind of magic toward which they naturally gravitate and provides the foundation from which their abilities develop. A Mage learns to understand this affinity, exploring what can be achieved through it and discovering increasingly sophisticated ways of expressing it.

Their education may take many forms. Some Mages are entirely self-taught, while others learn from another magically gifted individual. Some may eventually seek formal instruction from a college, institution, household, or other organization devoted to the study of magic. Such affiliations must be compatible with the established world of Ixoria, but they may form an important part of the character's proposed background.

Magic also manifests physically upon those who possess it. A Mage therefore bears some physical expression of their magical nature. The player should establish what this manifestation looks like and how, if at all, it relates to the character's affinity.

Finally, magic may have a history within the Mage's family. They may be the first known magically gifted individual in their lineage, or they may descend from others who possessed magical abilities. Such a lineage can influence how the character understands their abilities, how their family responds to them, and how much knowledge was available when their magic first began to express itself.

Ultimately, a Mage is defined by deliberate magical development. Their instinct upon encountering something they do not understand is to observe, investigate, experiment, and learn. Their magic is innate, but their mastery of it is earned.

Questions to Consider

  • When did your character first consciously experience their magic?
  • What happened during that first manifestation?
  • Did anyone else witness it?
  • How did your character initially understand or react to what was happening?
  • What is their magical affinity?
  • How did they discover the nature of that affinity?
  • How does their magic physically manifest upon their body?
  • Is this physical manifestation always visible, or does it become more pronounced when they use magic?
  • How did your Mage begin learning to control their abilities?
  • What kinds of experiments or exercises did they perform?
  • What important failures occurred while they were learning?
  • What discoveries about their magic are they particularly proud of?
  • What can they accomplish now that they were once incapable of doing?
  • Are they self-taught, or did someone teach them?
  • If they had a mentor, who were they and what was their relationship like?
  • Have they ever studied at a magical college, institution, household, or other organized place of learning? If so, describe what you have in mind.
  • What does your Mage still want to understand about their own abilities?
  • Are they the first known magically gifted individual in their family?
  • If magic exists within their lineage, who else possessed it?
  • How far back does the family's knowledge of its magical lineage extend?
  • Did those relatives possess similar affinities or physical manifestations?
  • How did their family react when the character's own magic began to express itself?
  • Has their pursuit of magical understanding ever caused an accident or harmed someone?
  • Is there something about their own magic that they fear, cannot explain, or have deliberately avoided experimenting with?

Warlock

A Warlock is someone born with a magical affinity who experiences and develops that magic primarily through instinct, emotion, and raw feeling. Like a Mage, their magical potential exists from birth and may reveal itself early or remain dormant until much later. The difference lies in how they relate to it once it begins to emerge.

Where a Mage seeks understanding, a Warlock seeks—or sometimes simply experiences—resonance. Their magic responds to what they feel. Fear, anger, grief, joy, desperation, love, exhilaration, or other intense emotional states may bring their abilities closer to the surface. A Warlock learns magic less by analyzing it than by recognizing how it moves through them and discovering how to reach the emotional and instinctive states that allow them to express it.

This does not mean that every Warlock is unstable, irrational, or constantly overwhelmed by emotion. A skilled Warlock may possess considerable self-control. Their mastery, however, is experiential rather than intellectual. They learn the sensations that precede an effect, the emotions that strengthen or distort it, and the point at which surrendering to an impulse becomes either powerful or dangerous.

For many Warlocks, a defining magical experience may be connected to trauma, fear, loss, violence, or another profoundly negative event. Magic may have erupted at a moment when the character was pushed beyond what they believed they could endure. Such trauma is not required, however. A Warlock's awakening might equally have occurred through wonder, joy, love, exhilaration, or another moment of extraordinary intensity. What matters is that their relationship with magic is rooted in lived emotional experience.

Every Warlock possesses an innate magical affinity, just as a Mage does, and their magic manifests physically upon them. The player should establish both the nature of that affinity and the visible or otherwise physical signs through which their magical nature expresses itself.

Magic may also form part of the Warlock's lineage. They may be the first known magical individual in their family or descend from generations in which magical affinity has repeatedly appeared. That history may influence whether their abilities were feared, encouraged, anticipated, misunderstood, or treated as something entirely ordinary.

A Warlock is therefore not simply a Mage who refused to study. They represent a fundamentally different relationship with magic. A Mage attempts to understand magic from the outside inward; a Warlock learns it from the inside outward. Their greatest strength may lie in their willingness to feel and act instinctively, while their greatest danger may come when the emotions through which they reach their magic begin to overwhelm them.

Questions to Consider

  • When did your character first consciously experience their magic?
  • What happened during that first manifestation?
  • What were they feeling at the time?
  • Did anyone else witness it?
  • Was this first experience connected to a traumatic or otherwise life-changing event?
  • If so, how did that event shape the person they became?
  • If not, what intense experience first brought their magic to the surface?
  • What is their magical affinity?
  • How does that affinity physically manifest upon their body?
  • Is the manifestation always present, or does it change when their emotions or magic intensify?
  • Which emotions bring their magic closest to the surface?
  • Are there particular feelings that make their magic stronger, weaker, or more difficult to control?
  • Has your Warlock learned to deliberately summon the emotional state they need to use their magic?
  • What does using magic feel like to them?
  • Do they enjoy the sensation of releasing their magic, fear it, or experience both?
  • Have they ever completely lost control of their magic?
  • What happened, and who was affected?
  • Is there an emotion they are afraid to experience because of what their magic might do?
  • How has their relationship with their own emotions changed since their magic began to manifest?
  • Did anyone teach them how to manage their abilities, or did they learn entirely through experience?
  • Are they the first known magically gifted individual in their family?
  • If magic exists within their lineage, who else possessed it?
  • Did those relatives experience magic in a similarly emotional way, or did they approach it differently?
  • How did their family react when the character's magic first appeared?
  • Does your Warlock want greater control over their magic, or do they believe that too much control would diminish what makes it powerful?
