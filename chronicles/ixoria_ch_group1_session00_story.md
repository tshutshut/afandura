Saltmere climbs from the sea in tiers of pale stone, its lower walls crusted white by a salt that never quite washes away. Once it answered to the Aurelate; now it answers to no one but its guilds. The Tidewrights hold the harbour, the Brine Compact the salt pans and the nets, and the Guild of Hammer and Loom the workshops of the market ring. And at the main gate, high on the hill, where every traveller must pass, stands the hall of the Wayguard Wardens, a squat fortress of grey stone hung with dark green banners. Most people simply call them the Grey Cloaks.

The morning came bright and clear over the city, with only a few clouds drifting in from the sea. The air tasted of salt and iodine, gulls wheeled and cried above the rooftops, and merchants were already calling out their wares to anyone who came through the gate. Five strangers woke in or around the city that day with no reason to think of one another. Before the day was out, the road would bind them together, though none of them knew it yet.

The first of them sat on a quiet stretch of the shore, far from the noise of the streets. His name was Valhir. For a few weeks now the Borru had been coming and going from the city, ever since the road had carried him away from his homeland, and he had lived as he could: foraging, hunting, and above all fishing, for there was no meal in the world he loved more than fish. This spot by the water had become his own. Yet today his heart was not in it. Beneath his wide straw hat, in his plain burlap tunic, he sat gazing at the sky and the endless water while his thoughts wandered somewhere far away, and it was only the trembling tip of his thick fishing rod that ever called him back. The sea had not been generous. A couple of sardines, an eel, a single sea bass and one other fish lay together at the bottom of his basket, a meagre reward for a long morning.

High above him, at the main gate, the city was already awake, and it was through that gate that Picea entered Saltmere for the first time. She had been born a Borru and had become an Ilie, and it had been a long time since she had last seen the lands of her people. Now she was looking for something she had gone without for years: work, and a little stability with it. She was strong, she had experience, and surely somewhere in a city run by guilds there was an employer with use for a fighter like her. The first thing Saltmere showed her was the hall of the Grey Cloaks, standing right beside the gate, grey and heavy, its great iron door still shut and two long green banners hanging from its walls. Nearby stood a wall so thick with notices that the stone beneath had all but disappeared. Layer upon layer of paper clung to it, torn, rain-washed, bleached by the sun and crusted with salt, with a few fresh posters pasted over the rest.

Picea read what she could. A smuggler called One-Thumb Teodric was wanted, though his face had been drawn so badly it could have been anyone's. The Brine Compact had raised its price for salt, the new notice pasted carelessly over the old. Someone had lost a grey goat named Duchess and was offering a reward in fish. The Guild of Hammer and Loom wanted apprentices with strong backs and steady hands, and the Grey Cloaks' rates for escorted journeys inland had gone up as well. But it was the newest sheet that held her eye. It bore the Grey Cloaks' seal and called for volunteers, agents and sworn swords to investigate the inland roads, with fair pay and lodging. Her spirits lifted, and she went to take her place in the line.

It was a strange company she joined. At its head, two hard-faced mercenaries were arguing with one another, as they seemed to have been doing for some time. Behind them stood a nervous young man, a folder of papers clutched tight against his chest. And just in front of Picea was a human with one arm missing, who talked on and on in a deep, rumbling voice to anyone who would listen, and to those who would not. He had been a great fighter, he said. He could still stand guard, still do his duty, still mount a horse and ride it, and it was the horses he returned to again and again. No one in the line answered him, so he turned to Picea instead. Little of what he said made sense, but she did not mind him.

Not far from the line, just beside the gate, Zorro-Ru the Suli had spread out his wares, while carts rattled past and merchants called out their prices. Fireworks were his true trade, and he knew them better than most, though he also carried jewels and little trinkets to wear, and when buyers were scarce he got by with whatever he could. This morning that meant a small show for the passers-by. His fireworks hissed and crackled, spitting bright sparks into the air, a cheerful little spectacle that almost no one stopped to watch.

One who did was Zillard Verde. The Sketh had been in Saltmere for a couple of days, sent by someone at Orden College to track down an artifact, a magical vase of some kind, and though his leads were thin and uncertain, one of them had brought him here. Intrigued by the sparks, he wandered over, and the two soon fell to talking. Zorro-Ru did what any merchant would and tried to sell him some fireworks. They haggled back and forth over prices, yet each of them seemed a little wary of the other, and the talk led nowhere. All the while, from the line by the hall, a deep voice kept rumbling over the noise of the street. It was the one-armed man, telling the big Ilie in front of him, for what seemed the hundredth time, that he could still ride a horse, any horse, as well as any man alive. Zorro-Ru understood soon enough that this was no customer, and with some frustration he began to pack up his things, meaning to try his luck elsewhere in the city. Zillard turned his mind back to his mission. The last person rumoured to have held the vase was a Lion Folk, and if there was gossip to be had in Saltmere, it would be found in the market. So he set off down into the city, for the gate stands on some of Saltmere's highest ground, stopping again and again to ask whether anyone knew of a Lion Folk of some renown. The answers came slowly, and the streets wound more than he expected, but in the end they all pointed the same way: to the marketplace, and to a shop called the Flower Salt Mane, where a middle-aged Lion Folk named Toreb Saltwain sold salt of every grade and quality. Toreb, they said, was a member of the Brine Compact, the guild that handles the salt of Saltmere.

Tetryl came through the gate soon after, and he had not come for work. He lived much of his life apart from others and had little love for cities, yet he had made the journey all the same, for at his side hung a small cage, and in it lay an injured pigeon, his friend, in poor condition. All he wanted was someone who could help it regain its strength. He went straight to the guard standing before the great iron door and asked where he might find an apothecary or a healer. The guard did not answer. He did not so much as look at him. Frustrated, Tetryl turned away and tried his luck with the people waiting in line, and there he found the one-armed man, whose name, it turned out, was Rumbling Rick. Rick only grew more confused, rambling still about the fighter he had been and the horses he could ride, yet in the middle of it all he said one useful thing. He had seen a Borru about the city, one with a big straw hat who came and went from Saltmere, and who seemed to know a thing or two about animals. Perhaps that Borru could help the bird. It was very little to go on, but it was more than the guard had given him, and Tetryl set off to find him.

Not long after, the great iron door swung open, and out came a young woman in a spotless grey cloak. She paused at the top of the steps, drew in a deep breath of the salty air as though she had been handed the finest job in all of Ixoria, and came bounding down towards the line with a ledger under her arm and a smile that seemed too large for her face. Her name was Mira. She took the names of the two mercenaries and sent them inside, then the nervous young man, then Rumbling Rick, cheering each of them on as if they had already done something remarkable. Then she reached Picea, and stopped. "Oh!" she said, her eyes going wide. "A Borru! And an Ilie! Both at once! Oh, you don't see that every day, do you? That's marvellous. Truly, truly marvellous." She asked for a name, wrote it down with enormous care, and read it back like a title. "Picea! What a strong name. A wonderful name. Oh, the Wardens are going to be so lucky to have you." The compliments kept coming, each brighter than the last, and the sunnier Mira grew, the less at ease Picea felt. She answered in as few words as she could and let the young woman's cheer wash over her, until at last Mira gestured towards the open door. "In you come, then! It's ever so simple, I promise. We'll take your name, stamp a few papers, and then you'll meet the officer in charge, Brannoch Mull. He's going to be *so* pleased to meet you."

Zorro-Ru, still packing up his wares by the gate, had watched the whole scene, and something about it sparked his curiosity. He slung his things over his shoulder and followed them inside. The entrance hall was tall, cool and quiet after the noise of the street. A great painted map covered one wall, the roads between the Free Cities studded with small grey pins, and in the moment he spent studying it, Zorro-Ru learned a good deal about how far the Grey Cloaks' reach truly extended across Ixoria. At the end of the long counter stood an Oshi in a grey cloak, ash-feathered and silent, who watched him with large, unblinking eyes and wrote something in a small black book. Zorro-Ru turned instead to the old clerk at the counter, a bald man who stamped every paper in front of him without once looking up, and told him with perfect ease that the young lady had asked him to come in. The clerk did not doubt him for a moment, and Zorro-Ru slipped down the hallway after Mira and Picea, towards the officer's door.

Meanwhile, down by the water, Valhir had finished with the sea for the day. It was time, he decided, to cook his catch, and his instinct told him that the market district was where he ought to go. So he began the long climb up from the port, for Saltmere is built upon a slope, and made his way towards the heart of the city. Chance, it seemed, had its own plans for him, for his path crossed Tetryl's, and Tetryl recognised the big straw hat at once. Yet rather than walk up and greet him, Tetryl slipped into an alleyway, crouched among some bushes, and threw a small rock to catch his attention. The rock struck the basket of fish and bounced up onto Valhir's head. It did him no harm, but it did wake him from his daydream. Valhir looked down, pointed at the rock lying at his feet, and picked it up. Then, turning, he saw a man crouching not far away, walked over, and held it out to him. "You lost your stone."

The conversation that followed was a strange one, yet the two of them got along well from the start, so well that before long Valhir closed Tetryl's fingers around the little rock and gave it a solemn nod. "Stone of friendship now. You keep it. Always with you." Tetryl explained that he had come to the city looking for medicine, and that he had been told to seek out a Borru in a large hat. Did he have any herbs for a sick pigeon? Valhir had no herbs, only fish, but his instinct told him that the entrails of his sea bass might do the bird some good. With skilled hands he gutted the fish, took a small piece of its innards, and fed it to the pigeon, which ate it gratefully. They talked a while longer, until Valhir declared that it was high time to get on to the market, for what he needed now was very fine salt, and he knew just the place to buy it. Tetryl decided to go along with him, and perhaps share in the meal, and so the two of them set off together through the streets.

Back at the Grey Cloaks' hall, Mira knocked far too brightly on a door that did not answer, and opened it anyway. Beyond it lay a small office that seemed to have given up long ago. Papers rose from the desk in leaning towers, notices were pinned over notices on every wall, curling at their edges, and in one corner a plant had died in its pot and simply been left there. The air was close and stale, thick with the smell of old food and older ale. Only one thing in the room had been cared for. On the wall behind the desk hung a survey map of mountain roads, every pass and every bend drawn with fine, patient precision, the slopes traced in delicate contour lines, its frame without a speck of dust. Among all that neglect, it looked as though it belonged to another man entirely.

Behind the desk, sunk so deep in his chair that he might have been poured into it, sat Brannoch Mull, a stocky Amdir with an enormous drooping moustache and very little hair left on his head. He did not stand. He did not, at first, even look up. "Officer Mull!" Mira beamed. "New recruits! Isn't it wonderful?" Brannoch let out a long sigh, the sigh of a man who had heard that question many times before. "Wonderful," he said. "Another one." His eyes moved from Picea to Zorro-Ru, who had come in on her heels, and lingered there a moment. "Two, then. Fine. Name. Kindred. What you're good for. Quickly." He noted something down, slowly, and spoke to them as though he both wanted them to join and wished they would not, every word heavy with the bitterness of a man who had expected far more from his life than this little room. When he was done, he waved them towards the door. "Out back. The yard. Wait for her there. She'll test you." He paused, then added, almost against his will, "Do well, and I might have a mission for you. Might. And try not to break anything I'll have to write a report about."

The yard was a sad place. Packed earth, cracked flagstones and stubborn weeds stretched between grey walls, and everything in it looked as though it had been used for years and repaired for none of them. A row of battered training dummies leaned against the far wall, arrows still bristling from their chests and their sacking slashed open, one missing an arm, another standing on a single leg. Old climbing posts and a wooden frame with peeling paint stood among a few scattered boulders, and a frayed rope swayed from the frame. Then Mira came bounding out and spread her arms wide, as though presenting the finest training ground in all of Ixoria. "Welcome to the training yard! Isn't it marvellous? Now, don't you worry, it's just a small drill, nothing at all, so I can see what wonderful things you can do!"

The first task was to climb the rope. Picea could not get up it at all, and Zorro-Ru, for all his agility, missed his first attempt, perhaps out of nerves. "Brilliant start! Brilliant!" Mira cried, and to show them how it was done, she went up the rope as easily as climbing a stair and perched at the top, shouting down at them without pause. "You can do it! Hand over hand! Oh, look at you both! The rope believes in you, and so do I!" With every cheer, Picea's frustration grew. Zorro-Ru made it up easily on his next try, and Picea followed. "Yes! Yes! Splendid! Absolutely splendid!" To come down, Mira leapt from the top, turned a couple of pirouettes across two boulders, and landed lightly on her feet. Her recruits were not so graceful. Zorro-Ru managed the descent, though not steadily, while Picea slipped on the second stone and fell hard, unhurt but thoroughly embarrassed. "Oh, what a brilliant recovery!" Mira called, with complete sincerity.

Next came the targets. Picea drew her bow and loosed an arrow that struck its mark, though a little to the side. "A hit! A hit! Outstanding!" She tried a second, and this time missed entirely, her frustration getting the better of her aim. "Oh, that one had such spirit!" Zorro-Ru threw his dagger and hit his target, if not in any very impressive way, and Mira applauded as though he had split a hair at fifty paces. "Outstanding warriors, both of you! The finest I have seen, truly!" Then she clapped her hands. It was time for the two of them to spar, and she would join in herself before long. Their bout was an untidy affair. Zorro-Ru could not bring Picea down at all, for she was far bigger and stronger than him, yet her own kicks and shoves never came together as well as she wished. "Lovely footwork! Lovely! Keep it up!"

Then Mira joined the fight, and she proved surprisingly quick. A neat kick to the stomach caught Picea first, and Mira darted at Zorro-Ru next, but her kick missed, and Picea seized the moment and rammed her square in the back. Mira stumbled forward, turned, and beamed. "Oof! Oh, that was wonderful! You must teach me that one!" Zorro-Ru came at her as well, though his punch landed more like a slap. "What a strike! Fearsome! Oh, I shall feel that for days!" Her delight at every blow, given or taken, only made the whole scene more absurd.

Down in the market district, Valhir led Tetryl straight to a shop he knew well, the Flower Salt Mane. Its owner, Toreb Saltwain, was a middle-aged Lion Folk with a great mane that was losing a little of its colour and sheen, yet was still kept with obvious care. He was busy with other customers when they came in, but his face broke into a smile the moment he saw Valhir, and Valhir, never one to stand on ceremony, walked right past everyone else to talk to him. Valhir had been a loyal customer these last couple of weeks, and Toreb was glad to see him, and curious about the newcomer at his side, and soon the three of them were chatting. Valhir, of course, could talk of nothing but fish, how to cook them and how to eat them, and above all the proper salt they needed. Toreb was never happier than when talking about salt. His desk was a model of order, lined with little bags in every colour, each one neatly labelled, and from a pink one he took a small spoon of salt.

He turned first to the newcomer. "Open your hand," he said, and poured the salt into Tetryl's palm. "Now taste. Slowly. Tell me what it says to you." He spoke of it as another man might speak of fine wine. Tetryl tasted it and frowned. "It's... salty." Toreb waited. "Very salty," Tetryl tried again. "Like the sea, maybe. With a hint of... rock? And something... earthy?" Toreb's smile grew thin, and it was plain he was not convinced. So he poured a second scoop, this time into Valhir's broad palm. Valhir tasted it and closed his eyes, and the words came in short bursts. "Berries. Good. Very good." He licked his palm again. "Blackberry. Oak. Little bit of smoke. Good salt." With every word Toreb nodded harder, utterly delighted. "Yes. Yes! I knew it. You have the palate, my friend. You know your salt." When at last they came back to business and Valhir explained that all he wanted was a little salt to cook his fish, Toreb would not hear of payment. "Take it. It's on the house." Valhir could not have been happier.

They left the shop to look for somewhere to cook, and on the doorstep they ran straight into Zillard Verde, who had only now found his way there through the winding streets. The Sketh stopped short at the sight of Valhir. "Magnificent," he breathed. "Look at you. Those shoulders, those arms. Such a frame! Forgive me, but may I touch? Your hand, your arm, only for a moment?" Valhir agreed to shake his hand, and no more, which Zillard accepted with something close to reverence. "Such strength in it. Remarkable. Truly remarkable." Tetryl, standing beside him, earned barely a glance. They talked a little, and Zillard explained that he was looking for a Lion Folk, and that everyone he asked had pointed him to this very shop. Valhir knew exactly who he meant. "Sir Salt Lion!" That was what he called Toreb, and they were good friends. So back into the shop they went, this time with the Sketh in tow.

Zillard admired Toreb as well, the care he plainly took over himself, and then asked him about a magical artifact. Toreb only spread his hands. There were plenty of Lion Folk in Saltmere, he said, and he knew nothing at all about magic. He was a merchant and a member of the Brine Compact, nothing more. "But if it's answers about magic you're after," he added, "you might try the Red Pilgrim temple. Theirs is a faith built around magic. If anyone in this city can tell you something, it will be them." The vase meant nothing to him, and so the three of them left the shop together. It was Valhir who decided where they would go next. Zillard had mentioned a Suli selling fireworks by the main gate, and to Valhir the reasoning was perfectly simple. "Fireworks," he said. "Fire works. And if fire works, it can cook my fish." So the plan was settled, and the three of them began the climb back up towards the main gate to find this Suli fellow.

Back at the hall, the recruitment had come to its end. Picea and Zorro-Ru were members of the Grey Cloaks now, novices at the very bottom of the ladder, but members all the same. Each was given a grey cloak and a cartwheel pin, a small buckler to hang at the side, and the choice of a bow or a short sword. Mira had ushered them out through the great iron door, chattering all the while, and had just gone back inside to fetch Brannoch so that he could give them their first mission, when the other three came walking up to the gate. On the way, Tetryl's pigeon had grown stronger, and after one last piece of sea bass it seemed almost itself again. So, there at the foot of the hall, Tetryl opened the cage and lifted the bird into the air. "See you, Octo. Take care." Octo flew only as far as a nearby rooftop, where it settled and cooed down at him for a moment, as if to say thank you, before it rose again and flew off towards the forest.

It was then that Zillard caught sight of Picea, and stopped dead. If Valhir had impressed him, Picea left him spellbound. She was big and imposing, yes, but strong and well formed with it, and he could not take his eyes off her. "Oh. Oh, look at you," he said. "Such power. Such form. Please, may I? Only your hand. Only for a moment." Picea refused, flatly, yet still he could not stop staring. At the same time Valhir had planted himself in front of Zorro-Ru with the question that mattered most to him. "You. The Suli. You have the fire that works?" He nodded at his basket. "If fire works, time to make it work. Fish needs cooking."

They were not alone on the steps. The silent Oshi from the entrance hall had come out onto the threshold, with his ash-coloured feathers, his grey cloak and his large, unblinking eyes, and now he came down to the three newcomers. His name, he said, was Hurin, and he spoke to them in a way none of them quite knew what to make of. "Three more, and none of them called," he said, looking from one face to the next. "The road keeps a record of everyone who walks it. Most never think to read it." And every time he spoke, and every time he looked at one of them, he wrote something down in his small black book, his pen moving steadily even while his eyes stayed on them. He asked nothing that seemed to matter, and answered nothing plainly, and still he kept writing.

Then Mira burst back out through the iron door, beaming brighter than ever. "Oh! More of you! Three more! Oh, how wonderful!" She clapped her hands at the sight of Valhir. "And that hat! Look at that hat! It's magnificent! Where did you ever find such a hat?" Not even Tetryl, dishevelled and still covered in moss and twigs, escaped her praise. "And you! So natural! So at one with nature! Oh, it's lovely, truly lovely." Hurin did not wait to be greeted. He closed his book without a word, turned and walked away down the street. Mira called after him, waving. "Hurin! Have a wonderful day! We really must catch up, it's been ages since we last spoke!" He did not look back, and Mira was not troubled in the least.

In the middle of all the commotion, she remembered the oath. She drew herself up, pressed a hand to her heart, and had Picea and Zorro-Ru repeat it after her, line by line. "I swear by wheel, by shield, by cloak," she began, solemn as a priest, and the two of them echoed her. "To guard the road and help its folk." Her chin rose a little higher. "To brave the rain, the mud, the gloom." Her eyes had begun to shine. "To sweep the yard and use a broom." She nodded gravely at each word, as though this were the most important line of all. "To never nap while on patrol." Here her voice caught, just slightly. "To share my lunch, if not the whole." She was blinking hard now, and her lip had started to tremble. "And should I fall, as heroes do..." She had to stop and swallow, and for a moment it seemed she might not go on at all. By the last line she was in tears, so that "I'll write it up, in triplicate too" came out as barely more than a whisper. She dabbed at her eyes with the corner of her cloak and drew a long, shaky breath. "I wrote that myself, you know. Three whole nights. I think... I think it might be my finest work."

That was the moment Brannoch Mull came out, and he did not look pleased about it. He had been made to leave his office, and he had heard the end of the oath on his way down the hall. Now he found the steps of his hall crowded with strangers, a sobbing Mira, a Sketh gazing at one of his new recruits and a Borru talking about fish. "Mira!" he shouted, red in the face. "How many times? Stop reciting that thing! Nobody needs your poem! We have a motto. One line. It's on the wall in there." Then he barked it out like a drill sergeant, one word at a time, with a hard stop after each. "Watch. Ward. Deliver. That's all anyone has to say!" Mira stood very straight and said nothing, though her lip was still trembling. Brannoch turned on the rest of them. "Will you all be quiet!" he barked, and for a moment, everyone was.

He looked over the three newcomers with deep weariness. "And you lot? You want to join as well?" Only Zillard said yes, and it was plain enough why. He had only just met Picea and barely knew her name, yet that seemed reason enough for him to sign up with the Grey Cloaks on the spot. Valhir and Tetryl had no interest in it at all. Brannoch sighed. "Fine. A third one. No need for the drill. Mira, stop crying and fetch him a cloak and a short sword, like the others." Then he turned to his three new recruits and gave them their orders. "A Red Pilgrim turned up last night, not far outside the walls. Half dead. He was taken from his caravan months ago, on its way to the first site of the Red Line. Not here, mind you. It happened out near another city, a long way off. How he ended up at our gates, nobody knows. They've brought him to the Red temple here so they can care for him. Go there, find the Vowed who keeps the place, and get the full story. Then come back and tell me what's what. I want a report. And try to be quick about it."

It was the same temple Toreb had named not an hour before. Somewhere in the city, a man who had been missing for months lay waiting for them, and the road that had brought five strangers together that morning was not finished with them yet.
